The artificial intelligence revolution was supposed to be one of the biggest money-making opportunities in modern history. Technology companies are spending hundreds of billions of dollars on data centers, advanced chips and computing infrastructure to build the next generation of AI.

But behind the excitement, a financial problem is beginning to attract serious attention.

Some of America's biggest banks are reportedly looking to move parts of their AI-related lending exposure to other investors. JPMorgan Chase and Citigroup are among the financial institutions marketing portfolios of AI-related loans, according to a November 26 report by Semafor.

The development raises an uncomfortable question for Wall Street: **If the AI boom is such a massive opportunity, why are some of the banks financing it trying to move part of the risk elsewhere?**

This does not mean the AI industry is collapsing. But it suggests that the financial institutions supporting its rapid expansion are becoming increasingly careful about how much risk they want to carry.

---

## JPMorgan and Citi Are Looking for Ways to Reduce AI Debt Exposure

The reported move involves portfolios of loans connected to the artificial intelligence infrastructure boom.

According to Semafor's reporting, JPMorgan and Citigroup are among the banks exploring ways to offload some of their exposure to AI-related debt.

There are different reasons why a bank might do this.

Some financial institutions want to reduce the amount of risk sitting on their books. Others may be looking to free up lending capacity so they can finance additional business.

In certain transactions, banks are also considering a structure known as a synthetic risk transfer.

This is where things get particularly interesting.

Under a synthetic risk transfer arrangement, a bank can keep the original loan on its balance sheet while transferring some of the potential credit losses to outside investors.

In simple terms, the bank does not necessarily sell the loan itself. Instead, it shifts an agreed portion of the financial risk to someone else.

That distinction is important because moving risk does not automatically mean the bank has lost confidence in the borrower.

However, it does show how financial institutions are managing their exposure as AI-related borrowing becomes increasingly significant.

## The AI Boom Is Becoming an Expensive Financial Race

The global AI industry requires extraordinary amounts of capital.

Companies need enormous data centers packed with advanced processors, powerful networking equipment and sophisticated cooling systems. They also need access to reliable electricity and long-term infrastructure commitments.

All of this costs money.

Major technology companies are increasingly using debt markets alongside their existing cash reserves and equity investments to finance expansion.

The Bank of England's November 2026 Financial Stability Report highlighted the scale of the issue.

According to the report, the combined debt and off-balance-sheet contractual commitments of major hyperscalers exceeded $4 trillion.

That figure includes more than conventional borrowing. It also covers wider financial commitments, such as leases, guarantees and other contractual arrangements.

The Bank of England warned that these complex financing structures could make the distribution of financial risks across the wider system less transparent.

Its concern is that if expectations about future AI revenues weaken, some of these financial commitments could come under pressure.

In other words, the AI industry is not just competing to build better technology. It is also creating an increasingly interconnected financial system.

## Investors Are Starting to Ask a Difficult Question

For years, investors have been enthusiastic about companies building AI infrastructure.

The logic seemed straightforward: demand for AI computing would continue increasing, data centers would become more valuable, and companies providing computing capacity would benefit from long-term growth.

But debt investors are now looking more carefully at the numbers.

Can data center operators generate enough revenue to cover their financing costs?

Will demand for AI computing grow quickly enough to justify today's enormous investments?

What happens if construction projects are delayed or customers reduce their commitments?

These questions are becoming more important as borrowing requirements increase.

A November 9 Reuters report described growing caution among banks and credit investors financing AI infrastructure projects.

The report highlighted concerns about construction delays, borrower cash flows, customer commitments and the ability of companies to service their debt.

It also reported that some lenders were becoming more selective about new AI-related financing deals.

This is a significant change in sentiment for an industry that has attracted enormous amounts of investment.

## Is Wall Street Facing an AI Debt Bubble?

The comparison with previous financial bubbles is becoming harder to ignore.

The current AI boom has similarities with earlier periods when investors became extremely optimistic about a new technology and poured enormous amounts of money into building the infrastructure needed to support it.

But there is an important difference between a technology becoming overvalued and the entire financial system facing a crisis.

AI has genuine commercial applications. Businesses are already using it for software development, customer service, research, automation and data analysis.

The real question is whether the financial returns will arrive quickly enough to justify the enormous borrowing and infrastructure spending happening today.

If AI adoption continues growing and companies generate sufficient revenue, much of today's investment could eventually make financial sense.

However, if revenue growth falls short of expectations, heavily indebted companies and their lenders could face difficult decisions.

That is why investors are paying closer attention to the debt supporting the AI revolution.

---

## Could This Become Another 2008?

The comparison with the 2008 global financial crisis is dramatic, but it deserves careful examination.

The 2008 crisis was driven largely by risky mortgage lending, excessive leverage and complex financial products that spread losses throughout the banking system.

The current AI financing situation is different.

The risks are connected to technology infrastructure, data center construction, corporate borrowing and the financing structures supporting major AI projects.

There is currently no evidence that JPMorgan or Citigroup's reported efforts to manage AI loan exposure signal an approaching financial crisis on the scale of 2008.

However, there is one important lesson from the previous crisis: financial risks can become difficult to understand when they are distributed across multiple institutions and investment structures.

The Bank of England has also warned that increasingly complex financing arrangements could make it harder to assess where potential losses ultimately sit.

That does not make another 2008 inevitable, but it explains why regulators and investors are watching the situation closely.

## The Biggest Risk May Be Hidden Behind the AI Success Story

The AI revolution is still attracting huge investments, and the technology continues to develop rapidly.

But the financial system supporting this expansion is entering a more complicated phase.

Banks want to manage their exposure. Investors are becoming more selective. Technology companies still need enormous amounts of capital to build the infrastructure behind future AI services.

These developments do not necessarily mean the AI boom is ending.

Instead, they raise a bigger question about how the next stage of the industry will be financed.

If AI companies deliver the revenue and productivity improvements investors expect, today's infrastructure spending could become one of the most important investments of this generation.

But if the financial expectations get too far ahead of actual business results, the consequences could extend far beyond Silicon Valley.

**The world is watching AI companies build the future. Wall Street, meanwhile, is beginning to calculate how much that future will cost.**
