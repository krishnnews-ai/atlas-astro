Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has raised the possibility of using cryptocurrency and blockchain technology to help the country prepare for potential international economic sanctions, triggering an unusually direct response from the Bank of Israel and criticism from more than 100 economists and business figures.

In a televised interview, Netanyahu said Israel needed to prepare for the possibility of restrictions targeting its banking system. When asked whether cryptocurrency and blockchain could form part of an alternative financial arrangement, he indicated that they were options under consideration and said he had requested a plan.

The proposal has not been presented as a fully developed financial system, and there is no public evidence that Israel has launched a national cryptocurrency programme designed to circumvent sanctions. The lack of operational details has become a central issue in the debate.

The Bank of Israel has rejected the idea that cryptocurrencies can provide a reliable route around sanctions, urging the government to protect the country's economic relationships with major trading partners instead. Economists have also warned that financial isolation could damage investment, employment and Israel's technology sector.

The dispute highlights a broader question for the digital asset industry: can blockchain-based payments meaningfully reduce a country's exposure to international financial restrictions, or do the underlying legal, banking and commercial constraints remain decisive?

## What Netanyahu said about cryptocurrency and blockchain

Netanyahu's remarks came during an interview with Israel's Channel 10 economic television network, broadcast on Friday, October 23.

The prime minister said the country should prepare for possible banking sanctions and develop an economic framework that could operate under more restrictive financial conditions. When the interviewer raised cryptocurrency as a possible option, Netanyahu agreed. He also identified blockchain technology as another possibility and said he had asked for a plan to be prepared.

However, Netanyahu did not publicly identify which digital assets might be considered, how a proposed system would operate, which institutions would be responsible for it or when it could be implemented.

Those omissions matter. A political statement about exploring blockchain is not the same as a government-approved programme, and a proposed payment system is not necessarily capable of replacing the international banking relationships required by a modern economy.

The distinction is particularly important when discussing sanctions. Cryptocurrency transactions may provide alternative ways to transfer value, but that does not automatically remove the legal obligations of the people, companies or institutions involved.

**As of October 25, the proposal remains an idea under discussion rather than a confirmed, operational national crypto system.**

## Bank of Israel rejects crypto as a sanctions workaround

The Bank of Israel responded to Netanyahu's comments with a clear warning: cryptocurrencies cannot serve as a means of circumventing sanctions.

The central bank argued that Israel should focus on maintaining economic relations with its principal trading partners and preventing measures that could harm the country's economy. Its response shifted the debate away from the technical possibilities of blockchain and toward the wider economic and diplomatic consequences of potential sanctions.

The disagreement is significant because a functioning alternative payment network would require more than a blockchain and digital tokens. Businesses would still need to pay suppliers, receive revenue, exchange currencies, access liquidity and comply with applicable laws.

Even if a transaction is recorded on a public blockchain, the people and companies involved may remain subject to financial restrictions. Exchanges, custodians, banks, payment providers and other intermediaries may also apply sanctions screening and compliance requirements.

Moving a payment from a conventional banking channel to a blockchain does not, by itself, make a restricted transaction lawful or guarantee that it can be completed.

The central bank's position therefore reflects a practical concern: alternative technology cannot necessarily compensate for the loss of access to international financial institutions, markets and commercial partners.

The disagreement also raises a policy question about who should determine the country's response to possible financial restrictions. Netanyahu has expressed interest in exploring alternatives, while the central bank has argued that preventing sanctions and protecting established economic relationships should remain the priority.

## More than 100 economists warn of economic consequences

The proposal faced further criticism on Sunday, October 25, when 109 economists, business executives and former public officials expressed concern about the prospect of international sanctions on Israel's banking system.

According to reporting by *The Times of Israel*, the group warned that sanctions could have serious consequences for businesses, exporters, investors and the country's technology industry. The signatories included former senior central bank officials and prominent economists.

Their concerns extend beyond cryptocurrency itself. Restrictions affecting international banking could make it harder for companies to receive payments, finance trade, attract overseas investment and maintain relationships with foreign customers.

Israel's technology sector is particularly relevant to the debate because many technology businesses depend on international capital, cross-border payments and access to global markets. If those relationships became more difficult to maintain, the consequences could extend to hiring, business expansion and investment decisions.

The economists also warned that a prolonged period of economic isolation could reduce living standards and put additional pressure on public finances.

These are warnings about potential outcomes, not evidence that all of these effects have already occurred. The extent of any economic impact would depend on the scope of future restrictions, the countries imposing them, the sectors affected and the response from businesses and policymakers.

Nevertheless, the public criticism demonstrates that the proposal has become part of a much wider argument about Israel's international economic position.

## Can Bitcoin or stablecoins actually bypass financial sanctions?

The debate touches on a recurring issue in cryptocurrency policy. Digital assets can move value across borders without every transfer passing directly through a conventional bank, but that capability should not be confused with complete financial independence.

Bitcoin operates on a decentralised network, while stablecoins are digital tokens generally designed to track the value of a reference currency, such as the US dollar. Both can be used to transfer value, subject to the characteristics of the asset, the network and the services used to access it.

However, neither automatically replaces the broader financial infrastructure that international trade requires.

**1. Blockchain transactions can still be traced.** Many public blockchains maintain transaction records that investigators and analytics firms can examine. A wallet address does not necessarily reveal a person's identity on its own, but activity may be linked to individuals or organisations through other information.

**2. Access to exchanges and payment services can be restricted.** Users often rely on exchanges, custodians, banks and other service providers to acquire digital assets, convert them into conventional currency or use them for commercial payments. Those businesses may be required to comply with applicable sanctions and other regulations.

**3. Stablecoins have their own limitations.** Some stablecoin issuers can restrict or freeze particular tokens or addresses under their policies and applicable legal obligations. A token that tracks the US dollar is therefore not necessarily an independent substitute for access to the US financial system.

**4. International trade involves more than transferring money.** Businesses also need suppliers, customers, insurance, shipping, credit, legal services and access to markets. A digital payment rail cannot independently resolve every restriction affecting those relationships.

These factors help explain why the Bank of Israel rejected the idea that cryptocurrency could serve as a general solution to sanctions.

That does not mean blockchain has no legitimate role in cross-border finance. Distributed ledger technology can support payment experiments, settlement systems and financial services. The more limited and practical question is whether a particular system can improve payment efficiency while complying with the laws that apply to its participants.

That question is very different from assuming that a country can use cryptocurrency to neutralise international financial restrictions.

## Why the story matters to the global crypto industry

The controversy puts cryptocurrency at the centre of a geopolitical debate rather than a discussion limited to prices, trading volumes or investment returns.

Governments have explored digital assets and blockchain technology for different purposes, including payments, financial infrastructure and digital identity. At the same time, regulators have paid increasing attention to sanctions compliance, illicit finance and the role of crypto service providers in international transactions.

Netanyahu's comments bring these issues into direct conflict. The idea of an alternative payment system may appeal to policymakers worried about losing access to conventional financial channels. Yet the same proposal raises questions about compliance, transparency, international cooperation and the practical limits of decentralised networks.

For cryptocurrency companies, the distinction between a legitimate payment innovation and an attempt to evade legal restrictions is especially important. A government announcement does not automatically establish that an asset, exchange or transaction is exempt from existing rules.

The debate may also draw renewed attention to the differences between Bitcoin, stablecoins and permissioned blockchain networks. They are not interchangeable technologies, and their suitability for payments depends on factors such as liquidity, volatility, governance, access and legal obligations.

For now, there is no publicly confirmed technical design that allows analysts to assess Netanyahu's proposed system in detail. Without that information, claims that Israel is preparing to launch a national Bitcoin system, a new cryptocurrency or a fully independent financial network would go beyond the available evidence.

## What happens next?

The immediate question is whether the proposal develops into a formal policy initiative or remains a political statement about preparing for possible future sanctions.

Several developments would help clarify its status:

- **A published government plan:** Details about the institutions involved, the intended use cases and the legal framework would establish whether the proposal has moved beyond an initial discussion.
- **A response from financial authorities:** Further statements from the Bank of Israel or relevant government agencies could clarify how any proposed system would interact with existing financial regulations.
- **A defined technical model:** Information about the blockchain, digital assets, payment providers and settlement process would allow analysts to assess the system's practical capabilities.
- **A change in the sanctions environment:** The nature and scope of any future international restrictions would determine the financial challenges Israel would actually face.

Until such details emerge, the story should be treated as a developing policy debate rather than a confirmed cryptocurrency rollout.

## The bottom line

Netanyahu's suggestion that Israel explore cryptocurrency and blockchain technology as preparation for possible banking sanctions has exposed a divide between political efforts to find alternative financial channels and the central bank's warning that digital assets cannot simply bypass international restrictions.

The criticism from economists adds another dimension: the potential cost of financial isolation could extend far beyond the payment system itself, affecting investment, employment, trade and technology businesses.

For the crypto industry, the episode is a reminder that blockchain can change how value is transferred, but it does not automatically erase legal restrictions or replace the relationships that connect national economies.

The key development to watch is whether Israel produces a concrete, publicly documented plan — and whether that plan can demonstrate a legitimate and practical use case within the applicable legal framework.
