The debate over whether traditional banks and cryptocurrency companies face different standards of enforcement has returned to the spotlight after Bank of America was hit with more than $1.21 billion in regulatory penalties in the United States .

Changpeng Zhao, widely known as CZ and the founder of Binance, responded to the development by questioning why the bank's case had not resulted in the same kind of personal criminal consequences that he experienced in the United States .

In a post reported by crypto news outlets on October 14, CZ highlighted the absence of publicly announced criminal charges against individuals in the Bank of America enforcement action, contrasting it with the consequences associated with the Binance case .

His remarks have reignited a broader discussion about accountability in the financial system: when a large financial institution fails to meet anti-money-laundering requirements, should its executives face personal criminal consequences, or are financial penalties and compliance reforms sufficient?

The timing makes the comparison particularly striking. US authorities say Bank of America failed to report more than $5 billion in suspicious transactions in a timely manner. The bank admitted to willful violations of the Bank Secrecy Act, while regulators imposed substantial penalties and required additional corrective measures .

At the same time, Binance previously agreed to a $4.3 billion resolution with US authorities, pleaded guilty to federal charges, and accepted extensive compliance obligations. CZ himself pleaded guilty in connection with Binance's failure to maintain an effective anti-money-laundering programme and was sentenced to four months in prison .

The two cases are not legally identical. However, the different enforcement outcomes have raised questions about how corporate penalties, individual responsibility and criminal prosecution are applied across traditional banking and cryptocurrency businesses .

## What Did CZ Say About Bank of America's $1.2 Billion Fine?

CZ's response focused on what he described as the difference in consequences between the traditional banking sector and the cryptocurrency industry .

According to crypto news coverage, his post stated:

"No one went to jail, no house arrest, no deferred prosecution agreements, not even charged."

The statement was a criticism of the apparent absence of personal criminal consequences in the newly announced Bank of America enforcement action. It was not a court ruling establishing that banks and crypto companies are treated differently in every case .

CZ's argument draws on his own experience with US authorities. Binance reached a multibillion-dollar settlement and pleaded guilty to federal offences, while CZ pleaded guilty in connection with the company's anti-money-laundering failures and received a prison sentence .

For crypto industry participants, that contrast raises a straightforward question: why can a company face major regulatory penalties without a publicly announced criminal prosecution of an individual, while a crypto exchange founder can personally face criminal proceedings?

The answer requires examining the actual allegations, legal provisions, evidence and enforcement decisions in each case .

A large financial penalty does not automatically establish criminal liability for a company's executives. Likewise, a corporate settlement in one case cannot establish that the same legal outcome should apply to another business without considering the evidence and applicable law .

CZ's post has nevertheless brought the issue back into the public conversation at a time when traditional financial institutions and digital-asset companies are both facing increasing scrutiny over financial crime controls .

## Bank of America Faces More Than $1.21 Billion in Penalties

On October 13, 2026, the US Department of the Treasury announced enforcement actions imposing more than $1.21 billion in penalties against Bank of America. The actions involved the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN), the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) and the Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) .

The penalties relate to anti-money-laundering compliance failures, delayed suspicious activity reporting and apparent violations of multiple sanctions programmes .

The announced actions include:

- **$1.2 billion:** A FinCEN penalty relating to failures to maintain an adequate anti-money-laundering programme and report suspicious activity.
- **$750 million:** An OCC civil penalty, described by the regulator as the largest in its history.
- **$11.6 million:** An OFAC settlement over apparent violations of multiple sanctions programmes.

These amounts should not be added together as though they represent $1.96 billion in separate penalties. The OCC's $750 million penalty is credited toward the $1.2 billion FinCEN penalty, while the $11.6 million OFAC settlement is separate .

The distinction matters because the headline figure can otherwise exaggerate the total financial burden imposed through these particular actions .

### What Were the Alleged Compliance Failures?

According to the Treasury Department, Bank of America failed to report more than $5 billion in suspicious transactions in a timely manner. More than $1 billion of the activity involved national-security-related or other priority illicit-finance risks .

Regulators described shortcomings in the bank's transaction-monitoring systems, customer due diligence and internal processes for identifying and investigating potentially suspicious activity .

The Treasury also said the bank relied too heavily on external inquiries, including law-enforcement requests and referrals from other financial institutions, instead of using effective internal controls to identify suspicious transactions proactively .

The OCC said the bank's anti-money-laundering programme remained deficient despite a previous December 2024 cease-and-desist order requiring improvements. The regulator cited failures to correct long-standing weaknesses and delays in escalating potentially suspicious activity for investigation .

These findings are significant because anti-money-laundering systems are intended to help financial institutions detect and report transactions that may involve illicit funds .

Banks are not expected to independently prove every underlying crime before submitting a suspicious activity report. Their obligation is to identify relevant warning signs, investigate them appropriately and comply with applicable reporting requirements .

The Treasury's enforcement action states that Bank of America admitted to willful violations of the Bank Secrecy Act .

That admission is an important fact. However, it should not be confused with a criminal conviction of an individual executive .

## Did Bank of America Face Criminal Charges?

The distinction between a corporate regulatory penalty and a criminal prosecution is central to CZ's criticism .

The October 13 announcement describes civil regulatory penalties, a sanctions settlement and required corrective measures. The official releases do not announce a criminal charge against a named Bank of America executive as part of these particular enforcement actions .

That is the basis for the comparison circulating on social media .

However, three separate questions must be distinguished:

1. Did the bank admit to violations? Yes. The Treasury said Bank of America admitted to willful violations of the Bank Secrecy Act.
2. Were substantial penalties imposed? Yes. The announced actions exceeded $1.21 billion and included additional compliance requirements.
3. Did the announced actions result in a publicly identified criminal prosecution of an individual executive? The cited announcements do not identify one.

The third point does not establish that no investigation into any individual could exist, or that an individual could never be charged in a separate proceeding. It means the specific enforcement announcement should not be described as a criminal prosecution of a named executive .

The legal distinction is important. A civil penalty can punish regulatory violations and encourage corrective action, while a criminal prosecution requires a separate legal process and proof of the relevant criminal offence .

Whether the consequences in this case are sufficient is a matter for public debate. Whether a particular individual has committed a crime is a separate legal question that cannot be answered from the size of the corporate fine alone .

## Binance's $4.3 Billion Settlement: Why CZ's Comparison Matters

To understand why CZ's comments have attracted attention, it is necessary to revisit Binance's own enforcement case .

In November 2023, Binance pleaded guilty to federal offences as part of a US resolution worth approximately $4.3 billion. The US Department of Justice said the company had failed to implement an effective anti-money-laundering programme and had violated other legal requirements .

The Justice Department said Binance had prioritised growth, market share and profits over compliance with US law. The resolution required the company to pay penalties and undertake compliance improvements, including independent monitoring .

CZ also pleaded guilty in connection with Binance's anti-money-laundering failures. In April 2024, he was sentenced to four months in prison .

The contrast is therefore not simply that Binance paid a larger or smaller amount than a traditional bank. It concerns the legal form of the proceedings and the consequences for the company and its founder .

## Are Traditional Banks and Crypto Companies Held to Different Standards?

The question of regulatory consistency is not new. Traditional banks operate under established frameworks covering anti-money laundering, customer identification, transaction monitoring, sanctions and suspicious activity reporting .

Cryptocurrency businesses can also be subject to these rules, depending on their activities, location and applicable law. Exchanges that handle customer funds or provide regulated financial services may face additional obligations under federal and state law .

The central issue is whether authorities apply the law consistently and pursue individual accountability when the evidence supports it .

### 1. Corporate Penalties Are Not the Same as Personal Criminal Liability

A company can agree to a settlement or admit regulatory violations without every employee or executive being charged with a crime .

Individual criminal liability generally depends on the person's conduct, knowledge, role, intent and the available evidence. Prosecutors must establish the required elements of the relevant offence .

This principle applies to both banks and crypto companies. A large corporate penalty is not, by itself, proof that a particular executive committed a criminal offence .

At the same time, the absence of a publicly announced individual charge does not resolve whether the enforcement response was sufficiently strong .

### 2. The Legal Proceedings Can Be Different

Bank of America's announced penalties involve civil regulatory enforcement and an OFAC settlement. Binance's 2023 resolution involved guilty pleas to federal offences .

These different legal pathways help explain why the cases produced different consequences. A civil enforcement action and a criminal prosecution do not follow identical standards or procedures .

That distinction does not automatically settle CZ's broader criticism. It does mean that any fair comparison must examine the legal basis and evidence behind each action .

### 3. Corporate Accountability Still Matters

Financial penalties can impose substantial costs on institutions, affect earnings and require changes to compliance systems .

In Bank of America's case, regulators also required reviews of past transactions and the effectiveness of its anti-money-laundering controls .

Such requirements are designed to address the deficiencies identified by regulators. Whether they are sufficient depends on the effectiveness of implementation, subsequent compliance and any further findings .

Public debate about accountability can reasonably consider both corporate consequences and the responsibility of individuals who knowingly participate in, direct or conceal wrongdoing .

### 4. The Same Principle Should Apply Across the Financial System

The wider policy question is whether the severity of enforcement should depend on the nature of the misconduct and the evidence, rather than simply whether a business belongs to traditional finance or the cryptocurrency industry .

A consistent approach would assess comparable conduct using comparable legal standards, while recognising that the facts and legal obligations in individual cases may differ .

CZ's comments have brought this question into focus, but a conclusion that regulators systematically favour banks would require broader evidence across multiple cases—not just a comparison between two enforcement actions .

## What Does the $1.2 Billion Fine Mean for Bank of America?

Beyond the debate over criminal accountability, the enforcement action has practical financial implications for Bank of America .

Reuters reported that the bank expected the $1.2 billion charge to reduce third-quarter net income by approximately 17 cents per share. The report also noted that the penalty could weigh on investor sentiment .

The bank said it had invested billions of dollars in technology and thousands of employees to combat financial crime. It also stated that it was working to complete the corrective measures raised by regulators .

The financial effect of a penalty is only one part of the story. The institution must also address the underlying compliance weaknesses, complete the required reviews and demonstrate that its systems can identify and report suspicious activity appropriately .

For shareholders, the relevant considerations include the cost of remediation, potential operational changes, future compliance expenses and whether the enforcement action affects the bank's broader business performance .

The announcement does not, on its own, establish the bank's future share-price direction or imply that its normal banking operations will stop .

## What Could This Mean for Cryptocurrency Regulation?

The case arrives at a time when regulators continue to examine the relationship between digital assets, financial crime prevention and traditional banking .

Crypto companies have argued that regulatory requirements should be clear and consistently applied. Regulators, meanwhile, have emphasised that financial businesses must maintain effective controls to prevent their services from being used for illicit purposes .

The Bank of America action demonstrates that large traditional financial institutions can also face substantial penalties when regulators identify serious compliance failures .

Binance's earlier case demonstrates that crypto companies can face criminal enforcement, corporate penalties and personal consequences for founders under applicable law .

Taken together, the cases highlight the importance of evaluating the details of enforcement actions rather than assuming that one sector is always treated more favourably than another .

Three questions will remain important for the industry:

- Will regulators pursue individuals when the evidence supports personal criminal liability?
- Will financial institutions and crypto businesses face comparable enforcement standards for comparable conduct?
- Will penalties and compliance reforms lead to demonstrable improvements in the detection and reporting of suspicious transactions?

These questions matter beyond the immediate controversy because effective financial-crime controls are relevant to banks, payment providers, cryptocurrency exchanges and other financial intermediaries .

## What Happens Next?

The Bank of America enforcement action requires the institution to undertake remedial work, including reviews intended to identify suspicious transactions that may not have been reported appropriately .

The effectiveness of these measures will become clearer as the bank completes the required reviews and regulators assess compliance with their orders .

The wider debate about CZ's criticism will depend on how analysts, policymakers and the public assess the legal distinctions between civil penalties, corporate guilty pleas and individual criminal prosecutions .

For now, the verified facts are clear: Bank of America faces more than $1.21 billion in announced penalties and admitted to willful Bank Secrecy Act violations. Binance previously entered a $4.3 billion federal resolution, and CZ received a four-month prison sentence after pleading guilty in connection with Binance's anti-money-laundering failures .

The different outcomes explain the renewed discussion, but they do not alone prove that every bank and crypto company is treated differently under US law .
