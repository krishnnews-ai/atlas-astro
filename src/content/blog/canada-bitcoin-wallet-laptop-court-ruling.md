A Bitcoin wallet connected to one of Canada's most disturbing murder cases has become the subject of an unusual court decision.

A judge in Nova Scotia has ordered authorities to return a laptop containing a Bitcoin wallet to the family of William Sandeson, a man serving a life sentence for the 2015 murder of fellow university student Taylor Samson.

The reason behind the ruling is particularly interesting: prosecutors failed to establish that the Bitcoin was connected to the killing, a drug transaction or the proceeds of crime.

The decision does not change Sandeson's murder conviction. Instead, it addresses a separate legal question over whether authorities had sufficient grounds to continue holding the laptop and the digital assets associated with it.

The case also highlights a question that is becoming increasingly relevant as cryptocurrency becomes part of financial and legal disputes: when authorities seize a device containing digital assets, what evidence is needed to justify keeping it?

---

## Why Did the Court Order the Laptop to Be Returned?

According to reporting by Decrypt and Canada's Global News, Nova Scotia Supreme Court Justice John Bodurtha ruled that prosecutors had not demonstrated a sufficient connection between the Bitcoin and the 2015 murder.

The Crown had argued that the cryptocurrency could have been connected to the drug transaction involving Sandeson and Samson.

However, the judge found that the suggested connection was hypothetical rather than supported by evidence showing that the Bitcoin had actually been used in the transaction.

The court therefore found insufficient grounds to continue treating the cryptocurrency as property connected to the offence.

Sandeson had sought the return of personal belongings seized following his arrest in 2015. The laptop containing the Bitcoin wallet was among those items.

The ruling means the laptop is to be returned to his family, according to the reports.

---

## The Murder Case Behind the Bitcoin Dispute

The cryptocurrency dispute cannot be understood without the events that led to Sandeson's arrest.

In August 2015, Taylor Samson, a 22-year-old Dalhousie University student, went to Sandeson's apartment in Halifax for a planned cannabis transaction.

Prosecutors said the deal involved approximately 18 kilograms of cannabis and a proposed payment of C$40,000.

Sandeson shot Samson and later disposed of his body in the Bay of Fundy, according to the court proceedings. Samson's remains have never been recovered.

Sandeson was initially convicted of first-degree murder in 2017, but that verdict was overturned on appeal in 2020.

At his second trial in 2023, he admitted shooting Samson but argued that he acted in self-defence. A jury found him guilty of second-degree murder.

He is serving a life sentence and is expected to become eligible for parole in 2030.

The latest ruling concerns the seized property, not the underlying murder conviction.

## How Much Bitcoin Is Actually in the Wallet?

The value of the Bitcoin has become another point of interest in the case.

Sandeson previously said the cryptocurrency was worth approximately C$8,000 to C$10,000.

However, a lawyer representing Samson's family in a separate wrongful-death lawsuit suggested that the Bitcoin could be worth more than C$200,000 at current valuations cited in the reporting.

Those figures represent competing estimates, not a verified valuation of the wallet.

The exact amount of Bitcoin held in the wallet has not been publicly established in the reports reviewed for this article.

That uncertainty matters because the laptop's return and the value of the cryptocurrency are two separate issues. The court's decision does not establish how much Bitcoin is held on the device or what its current market value might be.

## Why Was the Seized Cash Not Returned?

The judge did not order every item seized from Sandeson's apartment to be handed back.

Approximately C$5,000 in cash found at the apartment was treated differently.

The court considered that money to be offence-related property and ruled that it should not be returned.

The distinction is important.

The court found insufficient evidence linking the Bitcoin to the criminal activity, but it reached a different conclusion regarding the cash.

In other words, the decision was based on the evidence relating to each asset rather than a blanket ruling that all seized property had to be released.

## What This Case Reveals About Bitcoin and Criminal Investigations

The case offers an interesting example of how digital assets can complicate criminal investigations and property disputes.

A laptop can contain several kinds of information: personal communications, internet activity, financial records and access to cryptocurrency wallets.

But the presence of a Bitcoin wallet on a seized device does not, by itself, prove that the cryptocurrency was involved in a crime.

Authorities must establish the relevant connection through evidence.

That distinction is especially important because cryptocurrency can be held for years, increase or decrease in value, and become part of separate legal proceedings.

The Sandeson case also shows why courts may need to examine digital assets individually rather than assume that every asset found during an investigation is automatically connected to the alleged offence.

---

## Does the Ruling Mean the Bitcoin Is Now Free From Legal Claims?

Not necessarily.

The court's decision addressed whether the Crown had established sufficient grounds to keep the laptop as offence-related property in the criminal proceedings.

It should not automatically be interpreted as resolving every possible claim involving the Bitcoin.

The cryptocurrency had previously featured in a separate wrongful-death lawsuit brought by Samson's family. The return of the laptop does not, on its own, establish the outcome of that civil dispute.

The precise legal status of any remaining claims would depend on the relevant court proceedings and orders.

---

## The Bigger Picture: Bitcoin Is Becoming Part of Real-World Legal Disputes

As cryptocurrency ownership becomes more widespread, digital wallets are increasingly appearing in criminal investigations, asset-recovery cases and civil disputes.

Unlike physical cash, Bitcoin is controlled through cryptographic keys and wallet access. A computer or hardware device may provide access to a wallet, but it is not necessarily the asset itself.

That distinction can create complicated questions when authorities seize electronic devices.

The Nova Scotia decision is a reminder that digital assets still fall within ordinary legal principles governing evidence, property and criminal proceeds.

For cryptocurrency holders, the case does not create a new right to recover assets in every investigation. It illustrates a narrower point: authorities must support the continued seizure of property with evidence connecting it to the alleged offence.

## The Bottom Line

A Canadian court has ordered the return of a laptop containing a Bitcoin wallet to the family of a man serving a life sentence for murder. The judge found that prosecutors had not established the Bitcoin's connection to the crime.

The ruling is not an overturning of the conviction, but an unusual example of how cryptocurrency ownership and criminal-property law can intersect.
