Russia's cryptocurrency mining industry may need to rethink how it operates as pressure on electricity infrastructure grows. Instead of measuring success primarily by the number of mining machines installed or the amount of cryptocurrency produced, an industry association wants projects judged by their broader contribution to the economy.

The proposal comes from the Russian Association of Crypto Industry and Blockchain, known as RAKIB. Its executive director, Alexander Brazhnikov, argues that mining should be evaluated as part of a larger economic system connecting energy production, computing infrastructure, industrial activity and the lawful circulation of digital assets.

The idea is significant because electricity access and operating costs can determine whether a mining project remains profitable. Russia has already introduced tighter power-connection requirements for mining facilities, adding another challenge for companies planning new operations.

RAKIB's proposal does not establish new government rules. Instead, it presents an alternative framework that the association believes could help mining projects become more economically productive while reducing pressure on electricity supplies.

## Russia Wants to Look Beyond Bitcoin Production

For many mining operations, the basic business model is straightforward: obtain electricity, run specialized computers and earn cryptocurrency in return.

But RAKIB argues that this approach does not tell the full economic story.

A mining facility may generate revenue without necessarily creating substantial benefits for the surrounding region. According to Brazhnikov's proposal, policymakers and investors should also consider how much a project invests locally, how many jobs it creates, how much tax it contributes and whether it generates demand for domestic equipment and industrial services.

The proposed model treats mining as one stage in a broader chain.

Electricity powers computing equipment, computing produces digital assets, and those assets can then be sold through lawful channels. Revenue may subsequently be reinvested in equipment, infrastructure or other economic activities.

The association's argument is that the entire chain should be assessed rather than focusing only on the final cryptocurrency output.

For investors, this could mean greater scrutiny of a project's complete business model. Access to electricity alone would not necessarily demonstrate that a mining operation delivers a strong economic return.

## Electricity Is Becoming a Bigger Challenge for Russian Miners

Energy availability is one of the central issues facing Russia's mining industry.

On October 6, 2026, the Russian government adopted Resolution No. 1300, which introduced new electricity-connection requirements for cryptocurrency mining facilities and related infrastructure. The measures took effect on October 9 and require qualifying new connections to use an interruptible, lower-priority electricity supply category. Under this arrangement, power may be interrupted when the electricity system requires it.

The rules reflect a difficult balancing act. Mining facilities can consume large amounts of electricity, but households, public services and other industrial users also depend on reliable power supplies.

For mining operators, the possibility of interruptions changes the economics of running equipment continuously. A facility must consider not only its electricity price but also how frequently it can operate, how much revenue it earns during available hours and whether its contracts account for interruptions.

This is where RAKIB's proposal for mining as a controllable electricity load becomes relevant.

Instead of expecting mining facilities to consume power continuously, the association argues that operators should be able to adjust consumption when the electricity system faces peak demand or limited capacity.

In principle, a facility capable of reducing demand at the right time could make use of available electricity without competing as aggressively with other consumers during periods of shortage.

However, the financial benefits would depend on the project's equipment, electricity contracts, operating schedule and local grid conditions. Not every mining facility would be able to adopt the same approach.

## What Is a Controllable Mining Load?

Traditional industrial facilities often require electricity according to relatively fixed production schedules. Cryptocurrency mining can be more flexible because operators can sometimes switch equipment off and resume operations later.

RAKIB wants this flexibility to become an explicit part of how mining projects are planned and assessed.

Under the proposed approach, miners would need to consider when electricity is available, how much it costs to connect to the grid and whether their operations could affect supply for households and established industries.

The association also argues that mining operators should demonstrate their ability to reduce consumption during periods of peak demand through suitable equipment and contractual arrangements.

Such an approach could help energy planners distinguish flexible computing operations from facilities that need uninterrupted electricity.

But flexibility is not a complete solution to energy shortages. Shutting down equipment can reduce mining revenue, while restarting operations and managing power demand may involve additional costs.

The commercial case would therefore depend on whether the value of flexible consumption outweighs the revenue lost during periods when equipment cannot operate.

## Russia's Different Energy Regions Need Different Strategies

RAKIB's proposal also rejects the idea that one mining model would work equally well across the entire country.

Russia's energy infrastructure varies considerably by region, making local conditions an important part of any investment decision.

The association outlines several possible approaches.

**Coal-dependent regions:** Mining projects should account for the economics of electricity generation, the need to modernize facilities and the environmental costs associated with coal-fired power.

**Gas-producing regions:** Distributed electricity generation at industrial sites could provide another option. Where technically and economically feasible, using waste heat could improve the overall efficiency of an installation.

**Hydropower regions:** Investors should account for seasonal changes in electricity availability rather than assuming that surplus power will always be available.

**Nuclear power regions:** Long-term planning should consider the needs of industry and high-performance computing instead of treating mining demand in isolation.

These are proposals for evaluating regional projects, not evidence that the suggested infrastructure has already been built or approved.

For investors, the practical lesson is that a low electricity price does not automatically make a location suitable for mining. Grid reliability, connection costs, local restrictions, equipment requirements and the expected operating schedule can all affect profitability.

## Can Bitcoin Mining and Artificial Intelligence Work Together?

RAKIB also addresses the relationship between cryptocurrency mining and artificial intelligence computing.

Both activities can require significant electricity and specialized infrastructure. That similarity has encouraged interest in whether existing mining facilities could also support AI workloads.

However, the association cautions against treating the two activities as interchangeable.

Bitcoin mining relies on specialized application-specific integrated circuit machines, commonly known as ASICs. AI computing typically requires different hardware configurations, often involving graphics processing units or other accelerators suited to particular workloads.

A Bitcoin mining machine cannot simply be redirected to run the same range of AI tasks as a GPU-based computing system.

Cooperation may still be possible at the infrastructure level. Mining operators and AI computing providers could potentially share suitable facilities, energy infrastructure, technical personnel or regional development projects.

Whether such arrangements make economic sense would depend on the equipment, customer demand, financing requirements and operating costs of each project.

For Russia's mining industry, this distinction matters because the opportunity lies in evaluating complementary infrastructure rather than assuming that all energy-intensive computing facilities can perform the same tasks.

## RAKIB Proposes a National Mining Ecosystem Centre

Another part of the proposal involves creating a coordination centre called the National Mining Ecosystem within the ISKRA platform.

The proposed platform would bring together energy companies, industrial businesses, mining operators, equipment manufacturers, investors and regional representatives.

The aim would be to develop projects with clearly defined responsibilities rather than leaving each participant to solve infrastructure and commercial challenges independently.

According to Brazhnikov's proposal, the centre could evaluate potential sites, build a portfolio of investment projects, prepare standardized solutions and support pilot programmes.

The proposal also envisages starting with a limited number of pilot sites. Their projected economic performance would then be compared with actual results, allowing successful models to be expanded and unsuccessful projects to be discontinued.

The centre is not being presented as a new cryptocurrency exchange or government regulator. It would instead serve as a coordination mechanism for projects and industry participants.

Its practical impact would depend on whether the proposal gains support, whether the platform is established and whether the participating companies can turn the concept into commercially viable projects.

## What Would the Proposal Mean for Mining Investors?

If a model of this kind were adopted by participating regions or reflected in future industry arrangements, mining investors could face a more project-specific evaluation process.

Instead of relying primarily on electricity prices and expected mining revenue, investors might need to demonstrate broader economic benefits and a credible plan for managing power consumption.

That could influence several parts of a project's business case:

- **Electricity access:** Available capacity, connection expenses and interruption conditions would become central planning considerations.
- **Operating economics:** Revenue estimates would need to account for periods when equipment cannot run continuously.
- **Regional contribution:** Employment, tax payments, industrial contracts and investment could become more important measures of a project's value.
- **Infrastructure partnerships:** Cooperation with energy producers and industrial customers could help some projects make better use of available resources.
- **Regulatory exposure:** Investors would still need to comply with applicable mining rules and any restrictions imposed in their region.

None of these outcomes is guaranteed by RAKIB's proposal. The association's recommendations would need to be translated into practical arrangements before their effect on individual projects could be established.

For now, the proposal provides a framework for thinking about the industry's future rather than a confirmed change in mining regulations.

## The Bigger Question: Can Mining Deliver More Than Crypto Revenue?

The debate surrounding Russian Bitcoin mining reflects a wider challenge for energy-intensive digital industries.

Mining operators want predictable access to electricity and commercially viable operating conditions. Energy planners, meanwhile, need to protect supply for households and businesses while deciding how scarce capacity should be allocated.

RAKIB's proposal attempts to connect these interests by making the economic contribution of each project more visible.

Its central idea is that the industry should be judged not only by how much cryptocurrency it produces, but also by how efficiently it uses energy and what it contributes to the wider economy.

Whether that approach becomes influential will depend on its implementation. Pilot projects, transparent economic assessments and workable electricity agreements would be needed to demonstrate whether the proposed model delivers measurable benefits.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is RAKIB proposing for cryptocurrency mining in Russia?

RAKIB proposes evaluating mining projects by their net contribution to the economy, including investment, employment, taxes and demand for domestic equipment, rather than focusing mainly on mining capacity or electricity consumption.

### Has Russia officially approved RAKIB's new mining model?

The proposal reported by BeInCrypto is an industry recommendation, not a confirmed government policy change. Russia has introduced separate electricity-connection requirements for mining facilities, but those measures should not be confused with RAKIB's proposed economic framework.

### Why is electricity important for Russian Bitcoin miners?

Mining equipment consumes electricity continuously when operating. Power prices, grid availability, connection costs and potential interruptions can therefore have a substantial effect on operating revenue and profitability.

### Can Bitcoin mining equipment be used for AI computing?

Not all mining equipment can be repurposed for AI. Bitcoin ASICs are designed for a specific mining workload, while AI applications generally require different hardware. Facilities may be able to cooperate at the infrastructure level, but the technical and commercial feasibility must be assessed individually.

### What is the proposed National Mining Ecosystem Centre?

RAKIB's proposal describes a coordination centre within the ISKRA platform that would bring together energy companies, miners, manufacturers, investors and regional participants to evaluate sites and support pilot projects. Its establishment and future role should not be treated as confirmed unless further announcements verify them.

## Conclusion

Russia's cryptocurrency mining industry is facing a more complicated operating environment as electricity access, infrastructure costs and regional restrictions become increasingly important.

RAKIB wants the industry to respond by moving beyond a narrow focus on mining capacity and cryptocurrency production. Its proposed model would place greater emphasis on economic contribution, flexible energy consumption and coordination between mining operators and industrial partners.

The proposal is not yet an approved replacement for Russia's existing mining rules. Its significance lies in the debate it raises: whether energy-intensive crypto mining can become more sustainable commercially and more useful to the wider economy.

For miners and investors, the next developments to watch are any formal policy response, the establishment of proposed pilot projects and evidence showing whether flexible electricity use can improve project economics.
