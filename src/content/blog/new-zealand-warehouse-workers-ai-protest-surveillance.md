Artificial intelligence is increasingly changing how businesses operate. From automated warehouses and delivery systems to AI-powered analytics, companies are adopting technology to improve efficiency and reduce operational costs.

But a dispute at one of New Zealand's largest retail businesses has brought a more uncomfortable question into the spotlight: **When technology starts monitoring the workplace, where should companies draw the line?**

More than 300 employees at The Warehouse Group's distribution centres in Auckland and Christchurch staged protests on November 12, 2026, over concerns about automation, workplace monitoring and the future of their jobs.

The controversy has attracted international attention because of allegations that employees were being monitored over their bathroom breaks. The company has strongly rejected those claims, creating a dispute that highlights the growing tension between workplace technology and employee rights.

---

## What Triggered the Protests?

The protests were organised by Workers First Union, which represents employees at the retailer's distribution centres.

According to reporting by Radio New Zealand (RNZ), workers are concerned about the introduction of new technology, including stocktaking drones and automated equipment designed to handle warehouse operations.

The union says employees are worried that automation could reduce the need for human workers and weaken job security.

Some employees have reportedly spent between 15 and 30 years working for the company. For long-serving staff, the arrival of machines and automated systems has raised questions about whether their experience and existing roles will remain valuable.

The union has said it is not opposed to technological progress itself. Instead, it wants stronger employment protections, including opportunities for retraining and redeployment if automation changes existing jobs.

That distinction matters. The dispute is not simply about whether companies should use AI. It is also about what happens to the people whose work is affected by it.

---

## Bathroom-Break Monitoring Allegations Spark Controversy

One of the most widely discussed parts of the dispute concerns allegations about employee bathroom breaks.

Workers First Union organiser Hayley Courtney told RNZ that she had been pursuing concerns about bathroom monitoring for several months. She alleged that supervisors had followed employees to toilets and questioned them about their breaks.

The union also raised claims that some workers had experienced pressure around taking bathroom breaks, with reports of incidents in which employees allegedly avoided going to the toilet.

These allegations have generated significant public attention because monitoring employees' personal breaks raises serious questions about dignity, privacy and working conditions.

However, there is an important distinction between the allegations and what has been independently established.

The claims that workers were forced to use warehouse aisles as toilets have not been independently confirmed. The company has also strongly disputed them.

---

## The Warehouse Group Rejects the Claims

The Warehouse Group has denied tracking employees' bathroom visits and rejected the suggestion that its monitoring technology is AI-powered.

Chief People Officer Richard Parker told RNZ that the company was shocked by the union's allegations and said that bathroom use was not tracked.

He explained that the business uses scanning technology commonly found in distribution centres to monitor operational efficiency. According to the company, this technology has been used across the industry for decades and has no connection to bathroom visits.

The company also disputed suggestions that automation had already caused permanent job losses at its distribution centres.

Parker said no roles had been lost as a result of the stocktaking drones and stated that the business currently had three additional temporary roles in the relevant area.

The company argues that the drones improve stock accuracy, customer experience and workplace safety by reducing the need for employees to work at height.

It has also said it will consult with employees and consider retraining or redeployment where possible as technology changes the nature of warehouse operations.

The company's response is an important part of the story. At this stage, the bathroom-monitoring allegations remain disputed, and the retailer maintains that its technology is being used for operational purposes rather than tracking toilet breaks.

## Why Workers Are Worried About Automation

The dispute reflects a much bigger challenge facing businesses around the world.

Warehouses, factories, logistics companies and retailers are increasingly investing in automation. Robots can move products, drones can assist with inventory checks, and software can analyse operational performance.

These systems can improve productivity and reduce repetitive work. They can also create new technical roles and make certain tasks safer.

But the transition can be difficult for employees whose jobs may change or disappear.

For workers who have spent decades performing the same roles, the introduction of automated systems can create uncertainty about future employment, income and career opportunities.

Workers First Union has therefore called for stronger guarantees around employment security. Its position is that employees should have access to retraining and alternative roles when new technology changes the work they currently perform.

This is an increasingly important issue as AI adoption expands beyond office-based jobs into physical workplaces.

---

## The Bigger Question: How Much Should AI Monitor Employees?

The New Zealand dispute highlights a challenge that goes beyond one retailer.

Businesses have legitimate reasons to measure productivity, manage inventory and improve workplace safety. But employees also have legitimate expectations of privacy, dignity and fair treatment.

The difficult question is how organisations can use technology without creating an environment in which workers feel constantly watched or pressured.

There is also a difference between monitoring a business process and monitoring an individual employee's personal activities. Companies need clear policies explaining what information is collected, why it is collected, who can access it and how it is used.

Where AI is involved, transparency becomes even more important. Employees should be able to understand whether a system is automated, what decisions it influences and whether a human manager can review those decisions.

Without clear communication and safeguards, even technology introduced for efficiency can damage trust between employers and employees.

---

## AI's Workplace Expansion Is Creating New Challenges

The controversy comes at a time when businesses across multiple industries are exploring ways to use AI and automation.

Retailers are experimenting with automated inventory systems. Logistics operators are deploying robots to move goods. Manufacturers are using AI to detect defects, forecast demand and manage production.

These developments could make businesses more efficient, but they also raise questions about how the benefits of automation will be shared.

If technology increases productivity, workers and employers may disagree about who benefits most. Employees may seek better wages, retraining and job guarantees, while businesses may prioritise lower costs and faster operations.

The Warehouse Group dispute shows why these conversations need to happen before workplace technology becomes a source of conflict.

---

## What Happens Next?

The immediate dispute centres on employment protections, workplace monitoring and the future use of automation at The Warehouse Group's distribution centres.

Workers First Union is seeking assurances that employees will not be easily replaced as new systems are introduced. The company, meanwhile, says its technology is intended to improve efficiency and safety, and that it will consult with employees about changes to their roles.

The disagreement over bathroom monitoring remains unresolved in the public reporting available so far. The company denies tracking bathroom visits, while the union has raised concerns about how employees are treated.

The outcome could become an important example of how employers, unions and workers negotiate the introduction of AI and automation.

For businesses adopting similar technology, the lesson is clear: improving productivity is only one part of a successful transformation. Maintaining employee trust, protecting privacy and preparing workers for new roles are equally important.

**AI may be changing the future of work, but the debate in New Zealand raises a fundamental question: how can companies build smarter workplaces without making employees feel that they are losing control over their own working lives?**
