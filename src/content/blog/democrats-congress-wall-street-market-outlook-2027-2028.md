The balance of power in Washington has changed, and Wall Street is now facing a different political environment.

Democrats have won control of both the US House of Representatives and the Senate in the 2026 midterm elections, according to election results reported by the Associated Press. The outcome gives the opposition party control of Congress while President Donald Trump remains in the White House.

For investors, the result raises an important question: what happens when the president and Congress have different political priorities?

The answer could matter well beyond the next few trading sessions. Congressional control can influence legislation, government spending, taxation, financial regulation and oversight of the administration. These issues may shape business expectations and market sentiment over the next two years.

But there is another reason investors are looking closely at the result. Historical stock market data shows that the S&P 500 has recorded different average returns under different combinations of presidential and congressional control.

One historical comparison stands out: periods when a Republican president governed alongside a Democratic-controlled Congress produced the lowest average annual S&P 500 price return among the six political configurations examined by RBC Wealth Management.

That does not mean stocks are destined to fall. It does, however, provide a useful starting point for understanding the market questions facing investors as Washington enters a new period of divided government.

---

## Democrats Take Control of Both Chambers: What Changes Now?

The Democratic victories in the House and Senate represent a major shift in the political structure of the US government.

Before the midterm elections, Republicans controlled both chambers of Congress. That gave Trump's administration a more favourable legislative environment for advancing its policy priorities.

The new Democratic majorities change that equation.

Congress plays a central role in approving legislation, determining federal spending and conducting oversight of the executive branch. With Democrats controlling both chambers, the administration could face greater resistance to proposals that require congressional approval.

Potential areas of disagreement include:

- Federal spending and budget negotiations
- Corporate taxation and business policy
- Trade and tariff measures
- Healthcare and immigration legislation
- Financial services and cryptocurrency regulation
- Government oversight and investigations

The change does not automatically mean that every Trump administration proposal will stop. Some measures may still receive bipartisan support, while others could face delays or fail to secure enough votes.

For businesses and investors, the main issue is how the new congressional majority approaches these policy questions.

## The S&P 500's Historical Record Under Divided Government

One of the most closely watched parts of the election story is the historical relationship between political control in Washington and stock market performance.

RBC Wealth Management examined S&P 500 price returns from 1953 through 2025, grouping the data according to which party controlled the presidency, House and Senate.

Its analysis found that the S&P 500 recorded an average annual price return of 4.9% when a Republican president governed alongside a Democratic-controlled Congress.

Here is how the six political configurations compared:

| Political control | Average annual S&P 500 price return |
|---|---:|
| Republican president, Republican Congress | 13.3% |
| Republican president, split Congress | 7.3% |
| Republican president, Democratic Congress | 4.9% |
| Democratic president, Democratic Congress | 8.0% |
| Democratic president, split Congress | 17.0% |
| Democratic president, Republican Congress | 16.3% |

These figures provide historical context for the current political arrangement.

However, they should not be interpreted as a forecast for 2027 or 2028. The data covers different economic cycles, inflation environments, Federal Reserve policies and geopolitical events.

The figures also represent price returns rather than total returns, meaning dividends are excluded.

In other words, political control is one factor investors can study, but it cannot independently explain or predict the direction of the stock market.

## Why the 2006 Midterm Election Is Being Compared With 2026

The current political shift also brings attention to the 2006 US midterm elections.

During that election, Democrats gained control of both the House and Senate while Republican President George W. Bush remained in office.

The following years produced a dramatic contrast in market performance.

The S&P 500 gained approximately 3.5% in 2007 before falling about 38.5% in 2008, as the global financial crisis triggered one of the most severe market declines in modern US history.

The crisis was driven by deep problems in the housing market, mortgage-related financial products, banking institutions and credit markets. It was not simply caused by the change in congressional control.

That distinction matters when comparing 2006 with 2026.

The historical example shows that a political shift can be followed by very different market conditions in successive years. It does not establish that the same sequence will happen again.

Investors should therefore avoid treating the 2008 crash as a direct prediction for 2028.

## Why 2027 Could Become an Important Year for Investors

The first full year following the midterm election will provide an opportunity to see how the new congressional majority approaches its relationship with the White House.

Several developments could influence financial markets.

**1. Federal spending and budget negotiations**

Congress has significant influence over government spending and budget legislation. Disagreements between lawmakers and the administration could make negotiations more complicated.

Investors will be watching for potential disputes over spending priorities, federal borrowing and budget deadlines.

**2. Corporate taxes and business regulation**

Changes to tax policy or regulatory requirements can affect companies' costs, investment plans and expected profits.

Technology, banking, healthcare, energy and other major industries may pay close attention to proposals emerging from the new Congress.

However, proposals and political discussions should not be confused with enacted policy. Any actual impact will depend on the legislation that moves forward.

**3. Financial and cryptocurrency regulation**

Financial regulation could become another area of congressional debate.

For the cryptocurrency industry, the direction of legislation and regulatory oversight will be particularly important. Bills concerning digital assets, market structure and the responsibilities of financial regulators may face a different political environment.

A change in congressional control does not automatically mean that all crypto legislation will be blocked. The outcome will depend on the specific proposals, their support in Congress and the administration's response.

**4. Federal Reserve interest rate decisions**

Political developments are only one part of the market outlook.

The Federal Reserve's decisions on interest rates will remain important for equity valuations, borrowing costs and investor appetite for risk.

Persistent inflation could limit the scope for monetary easing, while moderating inflation could give policymakers more flexibility.

For investors, the direction of interest rates may have a more immediate effect on market valuations than the political headlines themselves.

## Could 2028 Bring a Different Set of Risks?

The 2028 US presidential election will introduce another major political event into the market outlook.

By then, investors will have had more time to assess how the Democratic-controlled Congress has worked with the Trump administration.

The political environment could influence expectations around taxation, regulation, federal spending and the next presidential transition.

But 2028 market performance will also depend on factors that have little direct connection to congressional control.

These include:

- Corporate earnings and profit margins
- Inflation and interest rates
- Employment and consumer spending
- Government borrowing and Treasury yields
- Global economic growth
- Geopolitical developments and energy prices

The 2008 comparison is a reminder that financial and economic risks can build independently of election outcomes.

A recession, banking-sector stress or major inflation shock could affect equities regardless of which party controls Congress.

For that reason, investors should monitor economic fundamentals rather than relying on political history alone.

## What the Democratic Majority Could Mean for Trump's Agenda

With Democrats controlling both chambers, the White House may find it more difficult to move major legislative proposals through Congress.

Democratic lawmakers will have greater influence over committee leadership, hearings, legislative priorities and congressional oversight.

The new majority can also scrutinise government actions and investigate matters within Congress's authority.

However, congressional control does not give either party unlimited power.

The president retains executive authority, can veto legislation and continues to direct the federal administration. Overriding a presidential veto requires a two-thirds majority in both chambers.

This means that negotiations, political compromises and disagreements could become a regular feature of the next two years.

For Wall Street, the important question is whether the resulting policy environment creates uncertainty that affects investment, hiring and corporate decision-making.

---

## Why Investors Should Look Beyond Election Headlines

Political developments can move markets in the short term, particularly when investors are uncertain about future policies.

But the long-term performance of US equities depends on a wider set of economic and financial conditions.

RBC Wealth Management's historical research offers an interesting comparison, but its figures do not prove that one party's control causes better or worse stock market performance.

Interest rates, inflation, corporate earnings, liquidity and global economic conditions can all influence equity prices.

The 2008 financial crisis demonstrates why it is important to understand the underlying causes of a market decline rather than attributing it to a single political event.

Investors should also distinguish between proposed legislation, enacted laws and policy changes that have actually taken effect.

---

## The Bottom Line: Washington Has Changed, but the Market's Direction Is Still Uncertain

Democrats' takeover of the House and Senate has changed the political environment for the final two years of Trump's presidency.

The new congressional majority could create greater scrutiny of the administration, complicate legislative negotiations and reshape debates over taxation, spending, financial regulation and cryptocurrency policy.

Historical S&P 500 data provides an additional reason for investors to examine the implications of divided government. But past averages cannot determine what stocks will do in 2027 or 2028.

The next two years will depend on how Congress and the White House interact, how the economy performs and how financial conditions evolve.

For Wall Street, the election has changed the policy landscape. The economic data will help determine what that change ultimately means for markets.
