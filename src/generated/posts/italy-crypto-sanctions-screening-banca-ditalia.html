Italy Tightens Crypto Sanctions Checks as Banca d’Italia Demands Stronger Transfer Controls

By CoinAINews Staff

Italy is putting stronger compliance pressure on the country's regulated crypto sector as Banca d’Italia reminds supervised firms that their systems must be capable of detecting relationships and transactions involving sanctioned individuals and entities.

The central bank published its communication on September 7, 2026, focusing on procedures for complying with European Union and national restrictive measures. It also pointed supervised intermediaries toward European Banking Authority guidance covering controls on customers and transfers of funds and crypto-assets.

The development was highlighted by CoinMarketCap, which reported that Banca d’Italia had ordered crypto service providers to screen transfers for sanctioned entities and strengthen internal controls against illicit flows.

But there is an important distinction behind the headline: Italy has not banned crypto transfers. The regulatory move is about sanctions compliance, screening systems and internal controls—not a blanket prohibition on Bitcoin, Ethereum or other digital assets.

What Did Banca d’Italia Actually Say?

Banca d’Italia's September 7 communication concerns compliance with EU and Italian restrictive measures.

The central bank said supervised intermediaries need adequate safeguards for detecting relationships and transactions involving sanctioned persons and entities. It specifically referenced EBA Guidelines 2024/15, which cover policies and controls concerning customers and transfers of funds and crypto-assets.

The guidance also addresses the configuration and calibration of screening systems, making the technical side of sanctions compliance an important part of the regulator's message.

In practical terms, regulated firms need systems capable of identifying potential sanctions exposure rather than relying on a basic or poorly configured screening process.

Why Crypto Transfers Are Under the Microscope

Crypto networks can move digital assets across borders without following traditional banking hours. That is one of their biggest advantages for legitimate users and businesses.

It also creates a compliance challenge.

When a regulated crypto provider processes a transfer, it needs to consider whether the customer, counterparty or transaction creates exposure to applicable restrictive measures.

That is why sanctions screening has become an increasingly important part of the infrastructure surrounding regulated crypto services.

The objective is not to stop ordinary crypto activity. The objective is to prevent regulated intermediaries from facilitating transactions that fall within applicable sanctions restrictions.

What Does Sanctions Screening Mean for Crypto Firms?

Sanctions screening generally involves comparing relevant customer and transaction information against applicable sanctions information and investigating potential matches.

For crypto firms, that process can be more complicated because digital-asset transactions involve blockchain addresses, pseudonymous accounts and transfers that can cross jurisdictions quickly.

A modern compliance operation may therefore combine customer information, transaction monitoring and blockchain analytics.

Does Italy Now Screen Every Small Crypto Transfer?

This is where the social-media headline needs some context.

CoinMarketCap summarized the development as Banca d’Italia ordering crypto providers to screen transfers for sanctioned entities. However, the central bank's official communication is framed around the adequacy of safeguards and compliance with restrictive measures, including the EBA's guidance on the configuration and calibration of controls.

Therefore, it would be too broad to describe the announcement as a brand-new Italian law stating that every single crypto transaction of every size must automatically be screened.

The stronger and more accurate takeaway is that regulated providers are expected to maintain effective sanctions controls and cannot treat crypto transfers as outside the scope of restrictive-measures compliance.

Is Italy Banning Bitcoin or Crypto?

No.

Banca d’Italia's communication does not announce a ban on Bitcoin, Ethereum, stablecoins or other cryptocurrencies.

It also does not say that ordinary users will lose access to crypto transfers simply because they are using blockchain networks.

The issue is compliance with applicable restrictive measures. If a transaction creates a legitimate sanctions concern, a regulated provider may need to investigate or take appropriate action under the relevant rules.

That is very different from a nationwide cryptocurrency ban.

Why EBA Guidelines 2024/15 Matter

Banca d’Italia's reference to EBA Guidelines 2024/15 is particularly important because it connects the Italian communication to a broader European supervisory framework.

The guidelines address policies, procedures and controls concerning restrictive measures, including customers and transfers of funds and crypto-assets.

They also emphasize the configuration and calibration of screening systems.

For crypto companies, that means compliance is increasingly becoming a technology problem as well as a legal one.

A provider needs systems that can process large volumes of transactions while identifying potentially relevant sanctions exposure without overwhelming compliance teams with meaningless alerts.

Why Blockchain Analytics Could Become More Important

Traditional sanctions screening often focuses heavily on names and other customer information.

Crypto adds another layer because transactions occur through blockchain addresses.

Blockchain analytics can help regulated firms identify links between addresses, transactions and entities that may present elevated risk.

But an important distinction remains: an address associated with a sanctioned entity is not necessarily the same thing as every address that has ever interacted with it.

That is why compliance teams need investigation and risk-based controls rather than treating blockchain exposure as automatic proof of wrongdoing.

What This Means for Crypto Exchanges and CASPs

The development matters most for crypto-asset service providers, exchanges and other regulated firms operating within Italy's financial system.

These businesses need to make sure their compliance infrastructure can support the applicable restrictive-measures requirements.

That can involve:

  • Maintaining current sanctions information.
  • Screening relevant customers and counterparties.
  • Monitoring applicable crypto transfers.
  • Reviewing potential sanctions matches.
  • Maintaining appropriate internal procedures.
  • Testing and calibrating screening systems.
  • Keeping compliance decisions and relevant records auditable.

The exact requirements can vary depending on the firm's activities, authorization and applicable regulatory framework.

Does MiCA Change the Situation?

MiCA—the Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation—is the European Union's framework for covered crypto-asset activities.

But MiCA compliance does not eliminate separate sanctions obligations.

A crypto business can operate within the European regulatory framework while still having to comply with applicable EU restrictive measures.

This is part of a broader shift in European crypto regulation: digital-asset companies increasingly need the same kind of compliance infrastructure expected from other regulated financial businesses.

Could Stronger Screening Affect Crypto Users?

Potentially, yes.

Users of regulated crypto platforms may encounter additional transaction reviews if a transfer generates a sanctions-related alert.

A review does not automatically mean that the user has done anything wrong. Screening systems can produce potential matches that require additional information or investigation.

For most users, the immediate impact is likely to be limited to compliance checks when a transaction or account triggers a relevant risk indicator.

Why Italy's Move Matters for European Crypto

Italy is not operating in isolation.

Across Europe, regulators are attempting to bring crypto services further into the regulated financial system.

That means the industry is increasingly being evaluated not only on technology and innovation but also on its ability to prevent money laundering, sanctions evasion and other forms of financial crime.

Banca d’Italia's latest communication fits into that wider regulatory direction.

The message is straightforward: blockchain-based money movement still has to operate within the rules governing regulated financial activity.

The Bigger Story: Crypto Compliance Is Becoming Infrastructure

For years, the crypto industry largely focused on wallets, exchanges, smart contracts and blockchain networks.

Now another layer is becoming equally important: compliance infrastructure.

As banks and regulated financial institutions move more assets and payments onto blockchain networks, they need tools capable of combining on-chain information with conventional customer and transaction data.

Italy's latest regulatory communication shows how that transition is happening at the supervisory level.

The future regulated crypto market will therefore depend on more than faster blockchains. It will also depend on whether firms can build compliance systems that work at blockchain speed without sacrificing regulatory controls.

What Happens Next?

Banca d’Italia's communication does not announce a new blanket enforcement campaign against specific crypto exchanges.

Instead, it reminds supervised intermediaries about the need for appropriate safeguards and points them toward the applicable European guidance.

The immediate implication is therefore operational: firms should review whether their sanctions controls are appropriately designed, configured and maintained.

Any enforcement action involving a particular crypto provider would be a separate matter.

Bottom Line

Italy is tightening the compliance spotlight on crypto transfers, with Banca d’Italia emphasizing the need for supervised firms to maintain effective controls against sanctioned individuals and entities.

The September 7 communication does not amount to a cryptocurrency ban. Instead, it reinforces the importance of sanctions screening, internal controls and properly configured compliance systems within the regulated financial sector.

For crypto companies, the message is becoming increasingly clear: moving assets on a blockchain does not remove the need to comply with sanctions rules.

And as digital assets become more integrated with European finance, the quality of that compliance infrastructure could become just as important as the blockchain technology underneath it.
