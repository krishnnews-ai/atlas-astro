Coinbase CEO Says SEC and CFTC Rules Could Be More Permissive After CLARITY Act Setback

By Coin Ai News Editorial Team |

The failure of the U.S. Senate to advance the CLARITY Act has shifted attention toward the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC), with Coinbase CEO Brian Armstrong saying regulatory action from the two agencies could be more permissive in some respects than the legislation that stalled in Congress.

Armstrong made the comments while speaking with Scott Melker after the Senate failed to advance the Digital Asset Market CLARITY Act. Meanwhile, former Treasury digital-assets counselor Tyler Williams offered his interpretation of why some Democrats who had previously supported the legislation ultimately voted against moving it forward.

Together, the comments highlight the two different paths now facing U.S. crypto regulation: continued action by federal agencies and the possibility of renewed congressional negotiations.

Brian Armstrong Says SEC and CFTC Rules Could Be More Permissive

Coinbase CEO Brian Armstrong said that rules eventually developed by the SEC and CFTC could be “arguably” more permissive in certain areas than the CLARITY Act would have been.

Armstrong's statement was an assessment of what could happen after the legislative setback. It was not an announcement that the SEC and CFTC had already adopted a replacement framework for the bill.

His comments came as the crypto industry began assessing what the failure of the legislation means for the regulatory environment in the United States.

Armstrong has argued that the two agencies already have significant authority under existing federal law and can continue working on clearer rules for digital assets even without Congress completing a comprehensive market-structure bill.

The distinction is important. A law passed by Congress can establish statutory definitions, responsibilities and requirements, while agency rules, guidance and other regulatory actions operate within the authority granted to federal regulators by existing statutes.

Senate Failed to Advance the CLARITY Act

The U.S. Senate failed to advance the Digital Asset Market CLARITY Act on September 15, 2026.

The procedural vote ended with 49 senators voting in favor and 50 voting against. The motion required 60 votes to clear the cloture threshold and allow the Senate to proceed with consideration of the legislation.

The vote therefore was not a final up-or-down vote on the completed CLARITY Act. Instead, it was a procedural vote on whether the Senate could move forward with the legislation.

Nevertheless, the result halted the bill's immediate path through the Senate and left the cryptocurrency industry without the comprehensive federal market-structure framework that supporters had been seeking.

The failed vote came after weeks of negotiations over several contentious issues, including regulatory jurisdiction, decentralized finance, stablecoin-related provisions and other areas of digital-asset policy.

Tyler Williams Gives His Interpretation of the Democratic Votes

Former Treasury digital-assets counselor Tyler Williams offered another perspective on the Senate vote during a discussion with Galaxy.

Williams argued that some Democrats who had previously supported the CLARITY Act voted against advancing it once it became clear that the legislation no longer had a viable path to passage.

According to Williams, voting yes under those circumstances could have carried a political cost for lawmakers because the legislation was unlikely to move forward regardless of their individual vote.

Williams' explanation is his interpretation of the lawmakers' decisions and should not be treated as an independently established motive for every senator who voted against the measure.

The Senate record establishes how lawmakers voted, but individual motivations can involve multiple factors. Public statements surrounding the vote also reflected disagreements over specific provisions and negotiations surrounding the legislation.

Why the SEC and CFTC Are Now in Focus

With comprehensive market-structure legislation stalled, the SEC and CFTC remain central to the development of U.S. crypto policy.

The SEC has authority over securities markets and securities-related activity, while the CFTC oversees commodities markets and certain derivatives. Determining how that authority applies to different types of digital assets has been one of the central issues in the U.S. crypto regulatory debate.

The CLARITY Act was designed to provide greater statutory clarity around those responsibilities and establish a broader framework for digital-asset market participants.

Without the legislation moving forward, crypto companies and investors will continue watching agency rules, interpretations, enforcement positions and other regulatory actions to understand how existing laws will be applied.

This is the environment in which Armstrong believes SEC and CFTC rules could potentially prove more permissive in certain areas than the stalled legislation.

Agency Action Can Continue Without CLARITY

The Senate setback does not mean that crypto-related regulatory activity has stopped.

Federal agencies can continue taking actions under existing statutory authority, although the scope of those actions remains limited by the laws Congress has already enacted.

That distinction is important for cryptocurrency businesses. Agency guidance or regulatory relief can address specific products or activities, but it does not necessarily create the same type of long-term statutory framework that Congress can establish through legislation.

For exchanges, token issuers, brokers, custodians and blockchain companies, the practical regulatory environment will therefore continue to depend on both existing law and decisions made by federal agencies.

Why the CLARITY Act Matters to Crypto Companies

The CLARITY Act was intended to address one of the industry's biggest regulatory questions: which federal agency should oversee different types of digital assets and crypto market activity.

For cryptocurrency exchanges and other market participants, regulatory classification can determine which rules apply, whether registration may be required and which compliance obligations a business must follow.

A comprehensive market-structure law could also provide statutory definitions and responsibilities that are more durable than individual agency interpretations or temporary regulatory measures.

That is why the Senate vote represents more than a single legislative setback. It leaves many of the industry's regulatory questions unresolved while the SEC and CFTC continue operating under their existing authorities.

Armstrong's View Does Not Mean CLARITY Is Permanently Dead

Armstrong's comments about potentially more permissive SEC and CFTC rules do not mean that Congress has permanently abandoned crypto market-structure legislation.

The September 15 vote prevented the bill from advancing at that stage, but it did not legally prevent Congress from considering similar legislation in the future.

Future negotiations could produce another version of the CLARITY Act or a different market-structure proposal. The timing and substance of any future effort will depend on congressional negotiations and the broader political environment.

For now, however, the immediate focus has shifted toward what federal regulators can do under existing law.

What Happens Next for U.S. Crypto Regulation?

The SEC and CFTC are likely to remain important sources of regulatory developments for the U.S. cryptocurrency industry while Congress considers its next steps.

Armstrong believes the agencies can make meaningful progress without waiting for Congress to complete CLARITY. His comments suggest that the industry could continue receiving regulatory guidance even after the Senate setback.

At the same time, agency action is not identical to congressional legislation. Regulatory rules can be challenged in court, modified by future administrations or affected by subsequent congressional action.

That leaves the crypto industry watching two parallel tracks: regulatory developments from federal agencies and the possibility of renewed congressional negotiations.

For market participants, both could have significant implications for exchanges, token issuers, decentralized finance projects, stablecoin companies and other digital-asset businesses operating in the United States.

Bottom Line

The failure of the CLARITY Act to advance in the U.S. Senate has changed the near-term path of crypto regulation, but it has not ended the debate over how digital assets should be regulated.

Coinbase CEO Brian Armstrong believes SEC and CFTC rules could ultimately be more permissive in certain areas than the legislation that stalled in the Senate. Former Treasury digital-assets counselor Tyler Williams, meanwhile, has argued that some Democrats who previously supported CLARITY voted against advancing it after the bill appeared to lose its path to passage, partly because supporting a measure that could not advance could carry a political cost.

Those statements represent the respective views of Armstrong and Williams. The established legislative fact is that the Senate failed to advance CLARITY in a 49–50 procedural vote and did not reach the 60-vote threshold required to proceed.

The next phase of U.S. crypto regulation will therefore depend on both agency action and future congressional negotiations. For Coinbase and other cryptocurrency companies, the SEC and CFTC's next moves may become increasingly important while the legislative debate remains unresolved.
