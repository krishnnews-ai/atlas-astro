Trump Announces ‘Biggest Oil Deal in World History’ With Venezuela — But Execution Will Take Years

President Donald Trump says the United States has reached a historic oil agreement with Venezuela — but the real test will be whether the deal can deliver on its promises.

U.S. President Donald Trump has announced what he describes as the “biggest oil deal in world history,” saying the United States has reached an agreement with Venezuela that would give Washington majority control over more than 65 billion barrels of proven oil reserves.

Trump made the announcement in a Truth Social post, saying the agreement was reached under his direction with Secretary of State Marco Rubio, Secretary of War Pete Hegseth, Venezuelan interim President Delcy Rodriguez, and private businesses.

According to Trump, the arrangement will come at no cost to U.S. taxpayers, increase America’s oil supply, more than double U.S. oil reserves and eventually help lower gasoline prices for American consumers.

The announcement has attracted immediate attention because of the enormous size of Venezuela’s petroleum reserves. However, major questions remain over the agreement’s structure, financing, legal framework and how quickly the promised investment could translate into additional oil production.

What Trump Says the Venezuela Oil Deal Includes

Trump’s announcement centers on U.S. control of more than 65 billion barrels of proven Venezuelan oil reserves.

Venezuela holds some of the world’s largest petroleum reserves, but its oil industry has struggled for years because of underinvestment, deteriorating infrastructure, sanctions, political instability and declining production capacity.

Trump argues that bringing greater American involvement into the sector could unlock that dormant potential.

In his announcement, the president said the agreement would substantially increase U.S. oil supplies and help drive gasoline prices lower for Americans over the long term.

The administration’s broader objective appears to be linking Venezuelan production with American energy security while encouraging private investment into Venezuela’s damaged oil infrastructure.

Reporting around the agreement indicates that development could involve 17 strategic oil fields and potentially attract close to $100 billion in private investment.

But the size of the reserve base should not be confused with the amount of oil that can immediately reach the market.

Why 65 Billion Barrels Matters

The size of the reserves involved is what makes the announcement particularly significant.

More than 65 billion barrels represents an enormous volume of crude oil. If substantial portions of those reserves can eventually be developed and brought into production, Venezuela could become considerably more important to global oil supply.

But there is a major difference between having proven reserves and producing oil at commercial scale.

Venezuela has some of the largest known oil reserves on the planet, yet production has remained far below the country’s potential because of years of inadequate investment and infrastructure problems.

That means the agreement, even if fully implemented, would not suddenly add 65 billion barrels of oil to the global market.

Instead, the real economic impact would depend on how quickly investment can restore production capacity.

The $100 Billion Investment Question

The proposed investment is one of the most important parts of the agreement because Venezuela’s oil industry needs much more than access to reserves.

The Washington Post reported that some of the oil fields involved have little existing infrastructure, while others have suffered from years of deterioration and equipment losses. Developing those fields could therefore require billions of dollars before significant additional production reaches the market.

That means private investors would potentially need to spend heavily on drilling equipment, pipelines, transportation systems, electricity, processing facilities and other infrastructure before they can generate meaningful returns.

The scale of the challenge also means that the headline investment figure should be viewed as a potential long-term commitment rather than money that will immediately enter the Venezuelan economy.

For Washington and private companies, the calculation will be relatively straightforward: Venezuela has enormous reserves, but turning those reserves into profitable production requires capital, infrastructure and political stability.

This is also where the difference between an announcement and an operating oil project becomes important.

Even if investment commitments are finalized quickly, construction, rehabilitation and drilling programs can take years.

Could the Deal Actually Lower U.S. Gas Prices?

Trump’s announcement puts lower gasoline prices at the center of the deal.

If Venezuelan oil production rises substantially, additional crude could eventually contribute to global supply and potentially put downward pressure on prices.

But the timeline is critical.

Restarting dormant oil fields is not something that happens overnight. Infrastructure must be repaired or rebuilt, production equipment must be installed, transportation networks must be restored and companies must be confident that they can operate safely and profitably.

That means American consumers should not necessarily expect the announcement itself to translate into cheaper gasoline at the pump in the short term.

The eventual effect would depend on how much additional crude Venezuela produces, how quickly that production reaches international markets and what global demand and supply conditions look like at the time.

A Deal With Major Legal and Political Questions

The agreement also faces questions inside Venezuela.

For decades, Venezuela’s oil industry has been closely tied to state control, and the country’s political and legal environment could play a major role in determining how much control foreign companies can ultimately receive.

The Washington Post reported that changes to Venezuela’s legal and hydrocarbons framework could potentially be required, while opposition to transferring control of natural resources to the United States could create additional obstacles.

That makes the agreement more complicated than a conventional commercial oil transaction.

Its ultimate success could depend not only on financing and engineering, but also on Venezuela’s political and legal institutions.

The Taxpayer Question

Trump has emphasized that the deal will come at no cost to American taxpayers.

However, the financing question may be more complicated than the headline suggests.

Developing Venezuela’s oil fields could require enormous upfront capital. While private companies may provide much of that funding, government-backed financing, guarantees or other forms of support could still become part of the broader structure.

The final economic exposure for the U.S. government will therefore depend on how the agreements are ultimately structured and how much public-sector support, if any, is committed.

That distinction will likely receive significant attention as more details emerge.

Venezuela Could Become More Important to the Global Oil Market

If the agreement survives its legal and political hurdles, its implications could extend well beyond the United States and Venezuela.

A significant recovery in Venezuelan oil production could change supply dynamics across the Western Hemisphere.

It could also strengthen U.S. influence over one of the world’s largest oil reserves and create a new energy relationship between Washington and Caracas.

For Venezuela, increased investment could provide badly needed revenue, employment and infrastructure development.

For the United States, greater access to Venezuelan production could strengthen its position in global energy markets.

But those benefits remain dependent on execution.

OPEC Could Also Face New Pressure

Another potential consequence is Venezuela’s relationship with OPEC.

Venezuela is a founding member of the oil-producing group, and increased U.S. influence over its petroleum industry could create a complicated relationship between Caracas, Washington and OPEC.

A significant increase in Venezuelan production could also complicate efforts by oil-producing nations to manage global supply.

If Venezuela eventually produces substantially more crude, the additional barrels could influence international oil prices depending on how quickly production grows and how much reaches export markets.

That makes the agreement relevant not only to U.S. gasoline prices, but also to the broader global energy market.

Why the Deal May Take Years to Deliver

The headline number — 65 billion barrels — is enormous.

But the more important number for the oil market is not the amount of oil sitting underground.

It is how many additional barrels Venezuela can actually produce each day.

That depends on investment, infrastructure, technology, security and political stability.

Venezuela’s oil industry has suffered from years of underinvestment, and some fields require significant rehabilitation.

Even with major financial commitments, rebuilding production capacity is a multi-stage process. Companies need to assess fields, repair equipment, restore infrastructure, drill new wells and establish reliable transportation and processing systems.

That is why the agreement’s long-term potential could be much more significant than its immediate impact.

The market will ultimately judge the deal by production numbers rather than the size of the announcement.

What This Means for Oil Markets

For global oil traders, the announcement creates both an opportunity and a question.

The opportunity is clear: Venezuela has enormous reserves that could eventually support higher production.

The question is how quickly that production can return.

If Venezuelan output rises significantly over the coming years, additional supply could put downward pressure on global crude prices.

If investment moves slowly or political and legal obstacles prevent large-scale development, the immediate impact could be much smaller.

Oil markets are therefore likely to focus on actual production data, investment commitments and project timelines rather than simply the headline reserve figure.

A Historic Announcement, But Execution Will Matter Most

Trump has called the agreement the “biggest oil deal in world history.”

The scale of the reserves involved certainly makes it one of the most consequential energy announcements of the year.

But the real significance will ultimately be measured by what happens next.

Can billions of dollars of investment actually reach Venezuela?

Can damaged oil infrastructure be rebuilt?

Can production increase substantially?

Can the agreement survive Venezuela’s legal and political challenges?

And can the additional supply eventually translate into lower energy costs for American consumers?

Those questions remain unanswered.

For now, the Trump administration has presented the agreement as a major step toward American energy dominance, while Venezuela sees it as a possible route toward rebuilding its oil industry.

The numbers are enormous, but the next phase will be about execution.

If the agreement delivers even a fraction of the production potential being discussed, it could reshape the energy relationship between the United States and Venezuela — and potentially alter global oil-market dynamics for years to come — but only if the deal survives the distance between announcement and actual production.
