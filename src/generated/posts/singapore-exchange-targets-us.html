Singapore Exchange Targets U.S. Institutions With Crypto Perpetual Futures

Top Crypto News 2026-09-14T08:52:14.726Z
Bitcoin NewsAltcoin NewsEthereum News



Singapore Exchange (SGX) is taking another major step into institutional crypto markets by opening its Bitcoin and Ether perpetual futures to eligible U.S. institutional investors. The move connects one of Asia's major regulated derivatives venues with U.S. trading firms and institutional capital, potentially giving professional investors another route into crypto derivatives outside traditional crypto-native exchanges.

The development is particularly important because perpetual futures have become one of the largest trading products in the digital-asset market. Unlike traditional futures, perpetual contracts do not have a fixed expiration date and typically use funding mechanisms to keep the contract price aligned with the underlying asset.

SGX launched its Bitcoin and Ethereum perpetual futures in November 2025. The exchange has since built meaningful activity around the products, with recent reporting putting cumulative trading volume at approximately $5.8 billion.

What Is Singapore Exchange Planning?

At first glance, the announcement may look like another crypto derivatives expansion. In reality, the significance is broader.

SGX is bringing Bitcoin and Ether perpetual futures into a regulated exchange environment that is already familiar to institutional trading desks. Rather than forcing traditional investors to move into an offshore crypto exchange ecosystem, the structure gives eligible U.S. institutions access to crypto perpetuals through SGX's established derivatives infrastructure.

The exchange's own rules identify its Bitcoin and Ethereum perpetual futures as products intended for Accredited Investors, Expert Investors and Institutional Investors.

That distinction matters. This is not simply a new retail crypto product. It is part of a broader effort to make crypto derivatives accessible through infrastructure designed for professional market participants.

Why Perpetual Futures Matter

Perpetual futures have become a core part of crypto market structure because traders can maintain long or short positions without dealing with a conventional futures expiration date.

That makes them useful for several institutional strategies, including:

  • Hedging Bitcoin and Ether exposure
  • Taking directional positions
  • Managing portfolio risk
  • Implementing basis and arbitrage strategies
  • Providing liquidity to other market participants

However, perpetual futures also introduce significant leverage and funding-related risks. A trader can lose money rapidly if the market moves against a leveraged position.

Why SGX's Move Is Significant

The most important part of the story is not simply that another exchange lists Bitcoin futures.

It is that a major traditional exchange is pushing a crypto-native derivatives product deeper into the institutional financial system.

For years, much of the world's perpetual-futures activity has been concentrated on crypto-native exchanges. SGX represents a different model: regulated exchange infrastructure, established institutional relationships and a derivatives market that already serves global financial participants.

This could make crypto derivatives easier to evaluate for institutions that have strict compliance, custody, risk-management and operational requirements.

Bitcoin and Ether Are the Initial Focus

SGX's initial crypto derivatives offering centers on Bitcoin and Ethereum, the two largest crypto assets by market importance.

That is a logical starting point for an institutional exchange. Bitcoin and Ether have deep global markets, established derivatives ecosystems and broad recognition among professional investors.

SGX's Bitcoin and Ethereum perpetual futures were launched in November 2025, giving the exchange a track record before expanding access to U.S. institutional participants.

SGX Is Trying to Bring Crypto Perpetuals Into Traditional Finance

The broader trend is easy to see.

Traditional exchanges, banks and financial infrastructure providers are increasingly building products around digital assets rather than treating crypto as a completely separate financial system.

SGX's strategy fits directly into that transition. The exchange is not trying to turn itself into a retail crypto app. Instead, it is using its existing derivatives infrastructure to offer institutional-grade access to crypto products.

That distinction could become increasingly important as more investment firms seek exposure to Bitcoin and Ethereum while maintaining traditional compliance and risk-management frameworks.

Why U.S. Institutions Could Be Interested

Institutional investors often care about more than the underlying asset.

They also need to consider regulation, counterparty exposure, operational controls, reporting, clearing arrangements and internal risk policies.

A regulated exchange such as SGX can therefore offer a familiar environment for firms that may be uncomfortable using less traditional venues for large derivatives positions.

For U.S. institutions specifically, the regulatory structure is particularly important.

What Does CFTC Regulation 48.10 Have to Do With It?

The U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission's Part 48 framework governs how foreign boards of trade can provide qualifying U.S. participants with direct access to their electronic trading and order-matching systems.

The CFTC explains that foreign boards of trade seeking to provide this type of direct access must apply for and receive an Order of Registration under Part 48.

Recent reporting says SGX has obtained the relevant CFTC authorization under Regulation 48.10, allowing eligible U.S. institutional investors to access its Bitcoin and Ether perpetual futures.

This is an important distinction. The latest development is therefore not simply an application waiting for a decision. The authorization has already been reported, while actual institutional onboarding and market participation can still take time.

Is SGX Really the First Major Traditional Exchange?

The phrase "first major traditional exchange" needs some context.

SGX is clearly an important traditional exchange operator bringing crypto perpetual futures into a regulated institutional environment. But saying it is the first traditional financial venue anywhere to offer any form of crypto perpetual product would be too broad.

The crypto derivatives market already includes regulated and institutional products across multiple jurisdictions and venues.

The more meaningful point is that SGX is creating another bridge between traditional institutional finance and crypto-native perpetual futures.

That bridge could become increasingly important as regulators and financial institutions attempt to integrate digital assets into established market infrastructure.

Could This Increase Bitcoin and Ethereum Liquidity?

Potentially, yes.

Opening a regulated venue to eligible U.S. institutions gives another group of professional market participants a way to trade Bitcoin and Ether derivatives.

More institutional participants can increase liquidity and market depth, but the relationship is not automatic. Liquidity depends on how many firms actually trade the contracts, how much volume they generate, and whether market makers provide competitive quotes.

If U.S. institutional participation grows meaningfully, SGX could become a more important liquidity venue connecting Asian and American trading activity.

The $5.8 Billion Number Matters

SGX did not enter the crypto derivatives market from zero.

Recent reporting says the exchange's Bitcoin and Ether perpetual futures have generated approximately $5.8 billion in cumulative trading volume since their November 2025 launch.

Bitcoin has accounted for the majority of activity, with recent figures indicating roughly 83% of average daily trading volume since inception and around 66% of open interest.

Those figures suggest there is already institutional demand for the products before the latest expansion of U.S. access.

What Are the Risks?

The expansion also comes with important risks. Greater institutional access does not make perpetual futures inherently safer.

1. Leverage Risk

Perpetual futures allow traders to control positions larger than the amount of capital posted as margin. A relatively small move in Bitcoin or Ether can therefore produce a disproportionately large gain or loss.

2. Market Risk

Crypto markets can experience sharp price movements, particularly during periods of high volatility. A leveraged position can be liquidated quickly when the underlying market moves against it.

3. Funding Risk

Perpetual contracts use funding mechanisms rather than conventional expiration. Funding costs can change depending on market conditions and positioning, affecting the economics of holding a position.

4. Counterparty and Operational Risk

Even within a regulated market structure, participants remain exposed to operational, clearing, technology and counterparty risks. Institutional traders therefore need their own risk controls rather than assuming regulation eliminates all potential losses.

5. Regulatory Risk

Crypto regulation continues to evolve across the United States and other major financial markets. Rules governing derivatives, market access, custody and digital assets can change over time.

What Happens Next?

The immediate question is no longer whether U.S. institutions can theoretically access the products. The latest reports indicate that SGX has received the relevant CFTC authorization.

The next stage is institutional onboarding and actual trading activity.

Recent reporting indicates that onboarding U.S. clients could take several weeks as firms complete operational and compliance requirements.

That means the real test for SGX will be how much additional volume arrives from U.S. institutions.

If participation remains limited, the announcement may have mainly symbolic importance. If major trading firms and institutional desks begin using the contracts at scale, SGX could become a more meaningful cross-border venue for Bitcoin and Ether derivatives.

What Could Happen to Crypto-Native Exchanges?

SGX's expansion does not necessarily mean traditional exchanges will replace crypto-native derivatives platforms.

Crypto-native venues still have advantages, including large global user bases, extensive token coverage and deep experience with perpetual contracts.

Instead, the market could become more fragmented.

Professional traders may use different venues for different purposes: regulated traditional exchanges for certain institutional strategies and crypto-native platforms for broader asset coverage or specialized trading opportunities.

Competition could ultimately push exchanges to improve liquidity, execution quality, risk management and product design.

SGX Could Also Connect Asian and U.S. Liquidity

SGX's geographic position is another important part of the story.

Singapore has developed into one of Asia's major financial centers, while the United States remains the world's largest pool of institutional capital.

A regulated derivatives venue connecting these markets could potentially make Bitcoin and Ether trading more continuous across global time zones.

For institutional traders, that can matter when managing positions around major market events, macroeconomic announcements or sudden changes in crypto volatility.

What Does This Mean for Retail Crypto Traders?

Retail traders are unlikely to become the direct beneficiaries of SGX's institutional expansion overnight.

The products are targeted at professional participants, and access requirements are different from those on many retail-focused crypto platforms.

However, retail traders can still be affected indirectly.

More institutional participation can potentially improve price discovery and liquidity across the broader Bitcoin and Ether derivatives ecosystem. At the same time, larger derivatives markets can also amplify short-term volatility when heavily leveraged positions are opened or closed.

The Bigger Trend: Traditional Exchanges Are Entering Crypto

SGX's move is part of a much larger transformation in financial markets.

Traditional exchanges and financial institutions are increasingly exploring crypto derivatives, tokenization and digital-asset infrastructure.

The important change is not simply that Wall Street or traditional exchanges are "buying crypto."

It is that digital assets are gradually being incorporated into established financial-market infrastructure.

That includes regulated derivatives, institutional custody, tokenized assets and new trading systems designed to connect traditional capital with blockchain-based markets.

What Comes Next for SGX?

Bitcoin and Ether perpetual futures may only be the beginning.

Recent reporting has pointed to further development of dated Bitcoin and Ether futures and options, which could give institutional traders additional tools for hedging and portfolio construction.

If SGX can attract sufficient U.S. institutional volume, the exchange could eventually build a broader digital-asset derivatives franchise around its existing infrastructure.

Why This Matters for Bitcoin's Institutional Future

The significance of the SGX development goes beyond one exchange or two crypto assets.

Every time a major regulated financial venue integrates a crypto product, the distance between traditional markets and digital assets becomes smaller.

Bitcoin and Ether are increasingly being treated not only as speculative digital assets but also as underlying instruments for professional derivatives strategies.

That does not eliminate volatility or investment risk. But it does show how deeply crypto markets are becoming connected to the global financial system.

Bottom Line

Singapore Exchange's expansion of Bitcoin and Ether perpetual futures to eligible U.S. institutional investors is an important development in the evolution of crypto derivatives.

The exchange launched its crypto perpetual products in November 2025 and has since generated billions of dollars in cumulative volume. The latest CFTC authorization opens another potential source of institutional liquidity and creates a stronger connection between U.S. trading desks and Singapore's regulated derivatives infrastructure.

The biggest question now is not whether the products exist. It is whether U.S. institutions will use them at sufficient scale to materially increase liquidity, open interest and trading activity.

If they do, SGX could become an increasingly important bridge between traditional finance and the global crypto derivatives market.
