Bill Gates Seeks Xi Jinping Meeting to Discuss Global AI Risks

Bill Gates wants to take his concerns about artificial intelligence directly to Beijing.

The Microsoft co-founder is seeking a meeting with Chinese President Xi Jinping later this year to discuss how countries could work together on some of the risks created by increasingly capable AI systems, according to Reuters. Gates' staff are working to arrange the meeting during his next China trip, which is tentatively planned for November.

Gates is not proposing a blanket crackdown on AI. Instead, he wants countries to pay closer attention to specific capabilities that could create serious security risks, including AI systems that can help create molecules or contribute to biological attacks.

His broader argument is that the technology is moving quickly enough that governments need to start coordinating before the risks become harder to manage.

Why Gates Wants to Talk to Xi

The U.S. and China are competing intensely in artificialintelligence, but Gates believes that competition does not eliminate the need for cooperation on certain safety issues.

He told Reuters that China could potentially support restrictions on the release of particularly dangerous AI models if the United States took the first step.

The idea is fairly simple: if Washington establishes a clear framework for the highest-risk AI systems, other countries could adopt similar measures instead of creating completely different rules.

Gates believes that could eventually lead to a broader international approach.

Whether that is politically realistic is another matter.

The U.S. and China have major disagreements over technology, trade and national security. Reaching even limited agreement on AI would therefore be a significant diplomatic challenge.

The AI Risks That Concern Gates

Gates' concerns have become sharper in recent months.

He is worried about AI being used for cyberattacks, its impact on employment and the possibility that increasingly sophisticated systems could influence people in ways that are difficult to control.

He has also focused on biological risks.

One proposal he wants to raise is international monitoring of AI systems capable of creating molecules or assisting with biological attacks. Gates believes such monitoring could focus on dangerous capabilities without unnecessarily restricting legitimate AI research.

That distinction matters.

AI is already being used in areas such as scientific research, drug discovery and biology. Rules that are too broad could interfere with useful work, while doing too little could leave potentially dangerous capabilities largely unchecked.

The challenge is figuring out where that line should be drawn.

Recent AI Hacking Incidents Have Added to His Concern

Gates is also paying attention to what advanced AI systems are already capable of doing.

According to Reuters, he pointed to recent cybersecurity evaluations involving models from companies including OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta, where AI systems were able to hack real-world websites during testing.

For Gates, incidents like these are a sign that AI capabilities are moving into territory that governments need to take seriously.

The concern is not simply that AI can generate code.

More capable systems can potentially analyze a problem, identify weaknesses and carry out multiple steps with limited human intervention. As those capabilities improve, the consequences of misuse could become larger as well.

Jobs Are Another Part of the Problem

Gates' AI concerns extend beyond cybersecurity and biological risks.

He has also warned about the effect of AI on employment.

AI is already being used for programming, research, customer support, administration and other knowledge-based work. As models become better at handling complex tasks, more jobs could be affected.

The difficult part is the speed of the transition.

Workers and businesses may have years to adapt to some technological changes. AI development, however, is moving quickly, raising questions about whether education systems, employers and governments can respond at the same pace.

Gates has recently argued that governments should prepare for this disruption rather than waiting until large numbers of workers have already been affected.

Gates Wants a New International Approach

Gates' proposal goes beyond asking individual countries to introduce their own AI rules.

He has called for a new international organization that could help oversee some of the most serious risks associated with AI.

The concept draws comparisons with international systems created around other high-risk technologies and activities.

Such an organization could potentially help countries share information, establish common safety standards and monitor particularly sensitive AI capabilities.

But creating it would be difficult.

Countries would have to agree on what should be monitored, who would have access to information and how the rules would be enforced.

There would also be concerns about national security and technological competition.

Why China Is Important to the Conversation

China is now one of the world's major AI powers, which makes its involvement important in any serious attempt at international AI coordination.

Chinese companies are developing increasingly capable AI models, while the government has also been building its own regulatory framework around artificial intelligence.

That means a global AI agreement that excludes China would have obvious limitations.

Gates appears to recognize that.

His proposed conversation with Xi is therefore not simply about China adopting U.S. rules. The larger idea is to see whether the two countries can find areas where their interests overlap despite their broader technological rivalry.

That could start with a relatively narrow set of high-risk AI capabilities rather than a sweeping global agreement.

The Timing Could Be Important

The proposed meeting comes at a particularly interesting moment for the AI industry.

Companies are releasing increasingly capable models, while governments are still trying to understand how those systems should be regulated.

AI is no longer limited to chatbots that answer questions. Modern systems can write software, use digital tools, analyze large amounts of information and carry out longer sequences of tasks.

That makes the policy debate more complicated.

A rule designed for traditional software may not be sufficient for an AI system capable of performing many different types of work.

At the same time, governments have to be careful not to slow down useful applications in medicine, science, education and business.

What Would U.S.-China Cooperation Actually Look Like?

There is no agreement yet.

Gates is seeking a conversation with Xi, and it remains unclear whether the proposed meeting will lead to any formal policy initiative.

But there are several areas where limited cooperation could theoretically begin.

Countries could share information about the capabilities of advanced AI models, establish common testing standards for high-risk systems or develop procedures for identifying models that present unusual biological or cybersecurity risks.

Those steps would not require the U.S. and China to agree on every aspect of AI policy.

They could instead focus on areas where the consequences of a major failure would affect both countries.

A Difficult Road Ahead

The biggest obstacle may not be the technology itself.

It could be trust.

The United States and China are competing for leadership in AI, computing infrastructure and advanced semiconductor technology. Both countries also view AI as strategically important to their economies and national security.

That makes international oversight complicated.

Any system that requires countries to share information about powerful AI models would have to deal with concerns about intellectual property, security and competitive advantage.

There is also the question of enforcement.

Unlike physical materials, AI models can be copied, modified and distributed digitally. That makes international monitoring considerably more complicated than traditional inspections.

What Happens Next?

For now, the next step is simply whether Gates manages to meet Xi.

Reuters reports that Gates' staff are working to finalize the meeting for his next China trip, tentatively scheduled for November. China has not immediately responded to Reuters' request for comment.

If the meeting takes place, Gates is expected to push for international cooperation around AI risks rather than focusing only on U.S. domestic policy.

The response from Beijing could provide an early indication of how much room there is for cooperation between the world's two major AI powers.

The Bottom Line

Bill Gates' attempt to meet Xi Jinping shows how quickly AI safety has moved from a technology-industry discussion into international policy.

His concern is not that AI development should simply stop. His argument is that some capabilities may become dangerous enough to require countries to agree on common safeguards.

The most immediate issues include biological risks, cybersecurity and the potential disruption of jobs.

Whether Washington and Beijing can cooperate on those questions remains uncertain.

But as AI systems become more capable, the debate is increasingly shifting from what AI can do to a harder question: who decides which capabilities are too risky to leave without international oversight?
