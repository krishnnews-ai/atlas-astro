Sam Altman Says AI Industry Did a “Terrible Job” Explaining Its Benefits

OpenAI CEO Sam Altman says the artificial int
elligence industry has a communication problem: companies building increasingly powerful AI systems have not done enough to explain what the technology can actually do for ordinary people.

Speaking with Bloomberg Television on Thursday, Altman said the AI industry has done a “terrible job” communicating the benefits of artificial intelligence. He also included OpenAI in that criticism, acknowledging that his own company has not done enough to explain the technology's potential.

The comments come as the AI industry faces growing public scrutiny over job disruption, massive data-center investments, energy use, cybersecurity and the concentration of technological power. Bloomberg reported that local opposition has blocked or delayed dozens of US data-center projects this year, adding pressure on AI companies to make a clearer case for their investments.

For Altman, that case should not rely only on futuristic promises. He believes AI companies need to show how the technology can give individuals more power to create, build businesses and accomplish things that previously required large teams or specialized expertise.

What Did Sam Altman Say About AI?

Altman's criticism was unusually direct because it was aimed at the broader AI industry as well as OpenAI itself.

“I think the industry has done a terrible job of this on the whole,” Altman said during the Bloomberg interview. He added that OpenAI had also done a bad job, although he suggested the company may have performed better than some competitors and worse than others.

Altman argued that the industry has not done a good enough job explaining how AI could expand creativity and entrepreneurship.

That is an important shift in emphasis. Much of the public conversation around AI has focused on automation, artificial general intelligence, job losses and the possibility of machines becoming dramatically more capable.

Altman wants another part of the story to receive more attention: what happens when powerful AI tools become available to millions of individuals.

Altman Says AI Should Give People More Power

One of the strongest themes in Altman's comments was individual autonomy.

He said it is very important to OpenAI that the technology gives people “more power and more autonomy, not less.”

The argument goes beyond productivity. If AI can help people write software, launch businesses, analyze information, conduct research or create products, then capabilities once restricted to large organizations could become accessible to individuals and small teams.

That could potentially change entrepreneurship in a significant way.

A person with a business idea, for example, may increasingly be able to use AI to handle tasks that previously required programmers, designers, researchers or large support teams. Whether that potential becomes a widespread reality remains an open question, but it is central to the optimistic case Altman is making for AI.

Why Ordinary People May Not Be Convinced Yet

The challenge for AI companies is that technological progress does not automatically translate into public support.

AI developers often discuss benchmarks, model capabilities, computing infrastructure and future breakthroughs. Those subjects are important inside the technology industry, but they do not necessarily answer the question most people care about: What does AI actually do for me?

For some workers, the first noticeable effect of AI may be automation or pressure to become more productive. For communities near proposed data centers, the discussion may be about electricity, water, infrastructure or local economic impact.

That creates a communications gap.

AI companies can see enormous long-term possibilities while the public may be dealing with much more immediate questions about employment, privacy, misinformation and control.

AI's Benefits Go Beyond Curing Cancer

Altman also argued that the industry's case for AI should go beyond pointing to major scientific achievements.

Breakthroughs in medicine, including the possibility of helping treat serious diseases, would obviously be significant. But Altman's broader argument is that AI should eventually improve everyday human capability as well.

That could include helping people become more creative, allowing entrepreneurs to build companies faster and giving individuals access to expertise that was previously expensive or difficult to obtain.

In other words, the value of AI does not have to come from one spectacular scientific breakthrough.

It could also come from millions of smaller improvements happening across businesses, schools, research organizations and individual lives.

Public Backlash Is Making the Message More Important

Altman's comments arrive at a time when the AI industry's infrastructure expansion is facing resistance.

Bloomberg reported that at least 75 US data-center projects worth roughly $130 billion were blocked or delayed by local opposition during the first three months of 2026, according to Data Center Watch.

That opposition illustrates why communicating the benefits of AI has become more than a public-relations exercise.

AI companies need enormous amounts of computing power to train and operate increasingly capable systems. That means their expansion increasingly intersects with local communities, electricity markets and infrastructure planning.

If communities do not see a clear connection between those investments and tangible benefits, resistance can become easier to understand.

AI Risks Are Still Part of the Conversation

Altman's comments should not be interpreted as saying that AI risks are unimportant.

The same technology that can increase productivity and creativity can also create new problems. Cybersecurity, privacy, misinformation, labor-market disruption and the control of increasingly autonomous systems remain major concerns.

In fact, the more capable AI becomes, the more important responsible deployment becomes.

The challenge for the industry is therefore not simply to promote AI. It is to explain its benefits while acknowledging legitimate concerns and demonstrating that users will have meaningful control over how the technology affects their lives.

Why Altman's Message Matters for OpenAI

For OpenAI, Altman's remarks are particularly significant because the company is one of the most visible organizations in the AI industry.

OpenAI has helped move generative AI from a specialist technology into a mainstream consumer product category. But greater visibility also means greater scrutiny.

The company's latest generation of AI systems is being developed at a time when expectations are extremely high. Users want more capable models, while governments, businesses and the public want stronger assurances around safety and accountability.

That makes communication increasingly important.

OpenAI cannot simply demonstrate that a model is more capable. It also has to explain why that capability matters and how people can use it without losing control over important decisions.

Creativity and Entrepreneurship Could Become AI's Strongest Selling Point

Altman's emphasis on creativity and entrepreneurship points toward a different vision for AI.

Instead of viewing AI primarily as software that replaces existing tasks, the technology could increasingly become a tool that allows people to attempt things they previously could not afford or did not have the skills to accomplish.

A small business could use AI for research, customer support and software development. An independent creator could use it to produce content and prototypes. A researcher could use AI to process large amounts of information more quickly.

None of those possibilities guarantees economic success. But they illustrate the broader argument Altman is making: AI could expand the number of people capable of turning ideas into reality.

The Bigger Question Is Who Benefits From AI

Perhaps the most important part of Altman's comments is the question of power.

If advanced AI is controlled by a small number of companies and governments, public concerns about concentration of power are unlikely to disappear simply because the technology becomes more capable.

Altman acknowledged that people can reasonably become concerned if they believe AI leaders want to concentrate power rather than distribute it.

That creates a fundamental test for the industry.

If AI is genuinely going to give people more autonomy, access to those capabilities will matter just as much as the capabilities themselves.

What Comes Next for the AI Industry?

The next phase of the AI race may involve more than building larger or more capable models.

Companies will also have to demonstrate practical value.

That means showing real examples of AI helping people solve problems, create products, learn skills, conduct research and build businesses. It also means being honest about limitations and risks.

Altman's comments suggest that the industry itself recognizes the gap between technological progress and public understanding.

The companies that successfully close that gap may have an advantage as AI becomes more deeply integrated into everyday life.

Bottom Line

Sam Altman says the AI industry has failed to explain its benefits clearly enough, and he includes OpenAI in that criticism.

His argument is that the public should hear more about how AI can increase individual creativity, entrepreneurship, power and autonomy—not just about futuristic breakthroughs or the risks associated with increasingly powerful systems.

The message comes at a critical time for the industry. AI companies are investing billions in infrastructure while facing growing questions about jobs, energy, safety and who ultimately controls the technology.

For Altman, convincing people that AI can improve their lives may now be almost as important as making the technology itself more capable.
