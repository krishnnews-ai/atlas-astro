Crypto Regulation Could Reverse Without Congress, Miller Whitehouse-Levine Warns

Top Crypto News 2026-08-18T12:30:08.412Z
Editor&apos;s ChoiceLearn crypto

 


The U.S. crypto industry has made significant regulatory gains over the past year, but those gains may prove difficult to preserve without legislation from Congress.

That's the warning coming from Miller Whitehouse-Levine, CEO of the Solana Policy Institute. His concern is that while federal agencies can deliver regulatory progress, those rules may be less durable than legislation passed by Congress—and a future administration could potentially reverse or modify them.

The Core Problem: Agencies Can't Offer Permanence

Whitehouse-Levine's warning comes as sweeping U.S. crypto legislation remains stalled in Congress, leaving federal agencies to fill the void. The industry-backed CLARITY Act, which aims to define whether crypto tokens should be regulated as securities or commodities, remains stalled in Congress, leaving its path forward uncertain.

That impasse has put the onus on the Trump administration's crypto-friendly regulators, particularly the Securities and Exchange Commission and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission. The SEC has been working on proposed exemptions that could ease securities requirements for certain crypto offerings, while the CFTC is moving ahead with discussions on additional cryptocurrency regulation.

"The agencies ... seemingly are ready to act, given that Congress has been unwilling or unable to do so," Whitehouse-Levine told Reuters.

But here's the catch: regulations introduced by federal agencies could face court challenges or be reversed by future administrations. The shift in regulatory priorities between administrations shows how quickly federal crypto policy can change.

And that's exactly what Whitehouse-Levine is worried about.

The "Gensler 2.0" Fear

Whitehouse-Levine's broader concern is that the industry's recent regulatory progress remains vulnerable without durable legislation. That concern was echoed by crypto executives who warned that a future administration could create a "Gensler 2.0" scenario.

The concern reflects how quickly U.S. crypto policy can change under a new administration. Under former President Joe Biden, SEC Chair Gary Gensler sued dozens of crypto companies, including Coinbase, Binance, and Ripple, arguing that many digital assets qualified as securities and should have been registered with the agency. Gensler famously described the crypto market as "rife with fraud, scams and abuse," comparing it to "the Wild West."

Some executives even fear that a future administration could install crypto hawks like Gensler once again.

Josh Riezman, chief legal and strategy officer at crypto trading firm GSR, put it bluntly:

"But then the next administration, depending on how that shakes out, we can be looking very much like a potentially Gensler 2.0 type scenario."

Progress That Could Be Undone

The industry has seen significant gains under the current administration. SEC Chair Paul Atkins, appointed in April 2025, has moved away from the enforcement-first approach of his predecessor. The SEC has stepped back from several major enforcement cases involving crypto companies, including Coinbase, Binance and Ripple. It has issued formal guidance on token classification through a joint memorandum with the CFTC.

The GENIUS Act, which provides regulatory clarity for stablecoins, became law in July 2025. But without broader market-structure legislation, the industry's regulatory gains remain vulnerable to future policy changes.

Whitehouse-Levine has repeatedly emphasized that these gains are fragile. Without permanent legislation, regulatory policy can shift with every new administration.

The Deeper Concern: Legal Ambiguity as a Weapon

Whitehouse-Levine's anxiety goes beyond who sits in the SEC chair. The deeper problem, as he sees it, is that "crypto better than any other industry unfortunately understands how legal ambiguities or interpretations can be weaponized against an industry."

Even if the industry secures its biggest legislative wins, the old legal frameworks—the Securities Act of 1933, the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, and the Commodity Exchange Act of 1936—are still on the books. A future administration hostile to crypto could simply reinterpret these laws in ways that once again make it impossible for the industry to operate.

Whitehouse-Levine, who previously worked at the DeFi Education Fund, has consistently argued for a clear regulatory distinction between centralized exchanges and decentralized finance software. Under a Gensler-like approach, however, this distinction would likely disappear, subjecting DeFi projects to the same burdens as centralized exchanges.

The Political Reality

The path forward isn't straightforward. While many Democrats support creating a regulatory framework for cryptocurrencies, they generally favor tougher safeguards against money laundering, fraud, and conflicts of interest than Republican lawmakers. Potential Democratic gains in the November midterm elections could give lawmakers greater power to scrutinize SEC and CFTC rulemaking efforts.

Meanwhile, traditional Wall Street firms that strongly oppose some SEC and CFTC crypto policies could also complicate implementation. The CME Group sued the CFTC in June over its decision to approve perpetual crypto futures, while industry groups have urged the SEC to place tighter limits on blockchain-based stock trading plans.

What Happens Next

For the crypto industry, the immediate question is whether regulators can provide meaningful relief while Congress remains deadlocked. The SEC and CFTC can move ahead with rules and exemptions, but those measures remain more exposed to litigation and future policy changes than a statute passed by Congress.

Reuters reported that the agencies are preparing to fill the gap left by stalled legislation, making the next phase of U.S. crypto policy increasingly dependent on regulators rather than lawmakers.

That does not mean the industry's recent progress is meaningless. It means the durability of that progress remains uncertain. If Congress eventually passes comprehensive market-structure legislation, it could establish rules that are harder for a future administration to reverse.

The Bottom Line

The central issue facing U.S. crypto regulation is no longer simply whether the industry will receive friendlier rules. It is whether those rules will survive a change in political leadership.

Miller Whitehouse-Levine's warning highlights the weakness of relying too heavily on administrative policy. The SEC and CFTC can provide meaningful regulatory relief, but congressional legislation would give the industry a more durable legal foundation. Reuters' latest reporting shows that agencies are already moving to fill the gap created by stalled legislation.

For crypto companies, investors and developers, that distinction matters. Regulatory momentum can change quickly. A permanent statutory framework would make that change far less disruptive.
